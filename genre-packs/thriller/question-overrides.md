# Thriller Question Overrides

Genre-specific answer options for the Crucible Planner questionnaire. These replace the default fantasy-oriented options when the Thriller genre pack is active. The question structure, numbering, and flow remain unchanged.

---

## Document 1: Crucible Thesis

### Q1.1 — The Burden

```
**CRUCIBLE THESIS — Question 1 of 10**

Every Crucible story has an external burden—something carried toward destruction or completion. In a thriller, the burden is what the protagonist KNOWS, POSSESSES, or PROTECTS that makes them a target. What form does this burden take?

A) Evidence that could bring down powerful people
   The protagonist has proof—documents, recordings, data—of crimes committed
   by people with the resources to make problems disappear. Possessing this
   evidence makes them a target. Releasing it will change everything.

B) A witness who must be protected
   A living person whose testimony or knowledge is the key to everything. The
   protagonist must keep them alive, keep them cooperating, and get them to
   the right place at the right time. The witness may not be willing.

C) Classified information that reveals a conspiracy
   Intelligence, government secrets, or proprietary data that exposes a hidden
   operation. The protagonist may have stumbled into it or been entrusted with
   it by someone who is now dead. Multiple parties want it.

D) A deadline before catastrophic event
   Something terrible is going to happen—an attack, a collapse, a release—and
   the protagonist is the only one who knows or the only one in a position to
   stop it. The burden is the CLOCK, not an object.

E) A person or identity the protagonist must maintain
   A cover identity, a double life, or a role the protagonist must keep playing
   to stay alive or to complete the mission. The burden is the performance itself
   and the cost of sustaining it.

F) Other (describe)
```

### Q1.2 — The Fire

```
**CRUCIBLE THESIS — Question 2 of 10**

The Fire is your protagonist's internal force that threatens to consume them. In thrillers, the Fire is psychological—the thing that drives them and destroys them simultaneously. What is its nature?

A) Obsession with truth that costs relationships
   The protagonist cannot let go of a question, a case, or a mystery. This
   obsession makes them brilliant at their work and impossible to live with.
   They sacrifice sleep, health, friendships, and love on the altar of knowing.

B) Paranoia that protects but isolates
   The protagonist sees threats everywhere—and they are often right. Their
   hypervigilance has kept them alive, but it has also made them unable to
   trust anyone fully. Every new relationship is filtered through suspicion.

C) Willingness to cross moral lines for justice
   The protagonist will lie, steal, manipulate, and possibly worse if they
   believe the cause is just. Each line crossed makes the next one easier. They
   are becoming the thing they are fighting, and they may not notice in time.

D) Past trauma that gives insight but destabilizes
   Something terrible happened to the protagonist—an attack, a loss, a
   betrayal—and it left them with both a wound and a weapon. Their trauma
   gives them insight into the antagonist's methods, but it also makes them
   unpredictable under pressure.

E) Addiction to danger and adrenaline
   The protagonist is most alive when they are most at risk. They seek out
   confrontation, take unnecessary chances, and feel empty during safe
   periods. This makes them fearless and reckless in equal measure.

F) Secret identity threatening to unravel
   The protagonist is not who they claim to be. Maintaining the deception
   requires constant vigilance and moral compromise. As the story progresses,
   the gap between who they are and who they pretend to be becomes
   unsustainable.

G) Other (describe)
```

### Q1.4 — Dark Mirror Connection

```
**CRUCIBLE THESIS — Question 4 of 10**

What connects protagonist and antagonist as dark mirrors? In thrillers, the mirror is often professional, institutional, or ideological—two people shaped by the same system who reached opposite conclusions.

A) Same agency or organization, different methods
   Both were trained by the same institution, held the same oaths, served
   the same mission. One stayed within the rules. The other decided the rules
   were the problem. They understand each other's tactics instinctively.

B) Same trauma, different responses
   Both experienced the same kind of loss, betrayal, or injustice. One
   responded by trying to fix the system. The other responded by trying to
   burn it down. Each believes the other chose wrong.

C) Same information, different conclusions
   Both have access to the same intelligence, the same evidence, the same
   truth. One concluded that exposure is the answer. The other concluded
   that control is the answer. The argument between them is genuine.

D) Same skills used for opposite ends
   Both are brilliant analysts, investigators, operators, or strategists.
   One uses their skills to protect. The other uses identical skills to
   exploit. Watching them work is like watching a chess match between
   mirror images.

E) Same idealism, corrupted versus maintained
   Both started with the same ideals—justice, public service, protecting
   the innocent. One kept those ideals despite the cost. The other let
   cynicism, power, or pragmatism erode them over time. The antagonist
   may not even realize how far they have fallen.

F) Other (describe)
```

## Document 2: Quest Strand Map

### Q2.3 — Antagonist's Stake

```
**QUEST STRAND — Question 3 of 7**

Why does the antagonist oppose the quest? In thrillers, the antagonist's opposition is usually rational—they have concrete things to lose.

A) Exposure of crimes or conspiracy
   If the protagonist succeeds, the antagonist's crimes come to light. This
   is not abstract—it means prosecution, imprisonment, loss of reputation,
   and the destruction of everything they have built. They will do anything
   to prevent exposure.

B) Loss of power or position
   The antagonist holds power—political office, corporate control, agency
   leadership, criminal territory. The protagonist's quest threatens to
   strip that power away. The antagonist may believe they NEED that power
   to accomplish something important.

C) Personal vendetta
   This is personal. The protagonist represents something the antagonist
   despises—a past humiliation, a moral rebuke, a reminder of who they
   used to be. The opposition is rational on the surface but driven by
   something deeper and more dangerous.

D) Ideological victory
   The antagonist is fighting for a vision of how the world should work.
   The protagonist's quest threatens that vision. The antagonist may
   genuinely believe they are saving the world, the country, or the
   institution from the protagonist's naive idealism.

E) Cover-up of past actions
   Something happened—years ago or recently—and the antagonist has been
   managing the fallout ever since. The protagonist's investigation is
   pulling at a thread that leads back to a buried body, a suppressed
   report, or a decision that cannot survive public scrutiny.

F) Other (describe)
```

## Document 4: Constellation Strand Map

### Q4.4 — Constellation Expansion

```
**CONSTELLATION — Question 4 of 7**

What types of allies join in Movement III? In thrillers, the protagonist's expanding constellation reflects the scope of the threat. Each new ally brings capabilities AND vulnerabilities.

A) Journalists or whistleblowers
   People who can make the truth public. They bring platforms, audiences,
   and the ability to create pressure the protagonist cannot create alone.
   But they have their own agendas, editorial pressures, and ethics about
   sourcing that may conflict with the protagonist's timeline.

B) Sympathetic insiders
   People still inside the institution, agency, or organization the
   protagonist is fighting. They provide intelligence, access, and cover.
   But they are at constant risk of exposure, and their loyalty may be
   divided between the protagonist and the institution they still serve.

C) Foreign intelligence assets
   Agents or contacts from other nations' services. They bring resources,
   different perspectives, and capabilities the protagonist lacks. But their
   interests are not aligned with the protagonist's—they have their own
   missions, their own handlers, and their own national priorities.

D) Tech specialists or hackers
   People who can access digital systems, decrypt communications, track
   movements, or build tools the protagonist needs. They operate in gray
   areas legally, which creates vulnerability. They may also have their
   own histories with law enforcement.

E) Lawyers or legal advocates
   People who can navigate the legal system, file motions, protect the
   protagonist's rights, or build a case. They bring legitimacy and
   institutional knowledge. But legal processes are slow, and the
   antagonist may have better lawyers.

F) Grassroots activists or citizen networks
   Ordinary people who are affected by the conspiracy and willing to act.
   They bring numbers, public pressure, and moral authority. But they are
   untrained, vulnerable, and may make mistakes that expose the operation.

G) Other (describe)
```

## Document 6: Dark Mirror Profile

### Q6.4 — The Dark Mirror's Offer

```
**DARK MIRROR — Question 4 of 9**

At Beat 26, what alternative will the antagonist propose? In thrillers, the Dark Mirror's offer is seductive because it is RATIONAL. It addresses a real problem with a solution that almost works.

A) "Join us and share the power"
   The antagonist offers the protagonist a seat at the table. Not as a
   subordinate but as a partner. They frame it as pragmatic: "You have
   seen how the system works. You know it cannot be fixed from outside.
   Help us run it properly." The offer includes resources, protection,
   and influence.

B) "Walk away and we will leave you alone"
   The antagonist offers a clean exit. No more running, no more threats,
   no more danger to loved ones. "You have proven you are dangerous enough
   to be respected. Take your life back. This was never your fight." The
   offer is credible because the antagonist has the power to enforce it.

C) "Here is the truth — now you understand why we did it"
   The antagonist reveals information the protagonist did not have—a
   deeper threat, a harder calculus, a context that makes the conspiracy
   look like the lesser evil. "We did terrible things, yes. But the
   alternative was worse. And now YOU know that too."

D) "Protection for your loved ones in exchange for silence"
   The antagonist threatens what the protagonist values most and then
   offers to protect it—IF the protagonist stands down. This is
   simultaneously a threat and a genuine offer. The antagonist may
   actually follow through on either the threat or the protection.

E) "We are not so different — and the real enemy is still out there"
   The antagonist redirects the conflict toward a third party. A bigger
   conspiracy, a more dangerous threat, a common enemy that makes their
   disagreement look petty. "Stop fighting me and help me fight THEM."
   The worst part is that this might be true.

F) Other (describe)
```

## Document 9: World Forge

### Q9.1 — World's Wound

```
**WORLD FORGE — Question 1 of 9**

What systemic problem does the Quest address? In thrillers, the "World's Wound" is not cosmic or magical—it is institutional, political, or technological. It is the rot at the heart of a system people depend on.

A) Institutional corruption at the highest levels
   The institutions meant to protect the public—government agencies, law
   enforcement, regulatory bodies—have been compromised from within. The
   corruption is not a few bad actors; it is structural. The people who
   should be fixing the problem ARE the problem.

B) Technology being weaponized against citizens
   Surveillance systems, data collection, AI, communications infrastructure,
   or weapons technology is being used against the people it was built to
   serve. The weaponization may be legal, which makes it harder to fight.

C) Foreign power infiltration
   A hostile nation, organization, or network has embedded itself in
   critical institutions—intelligence agencies, government offices,
   financial systems, media organizations. The infiltration is deeper
   and older than anyone suspects.

D) Corporate cover-up of public danger
   A corporation is hiding a product defect, an environmental disaster,
   a health risk, or a technology failure that threatens public safety.
   The cover-up involves regulatory capture, media suppression, legal
   intimidation, and potentially violence.

E) Deep state operating beyond oversight
   A faction within the government or security apparatus is conducting
   operations without authorization, accountability, or legal restraint.
   They may believe they are serving the national interest, but they have
   become a law unto themselves.

F) Other (describe)
```

### Q9.2 — Power Source

```
**WORLD FORGE — Question 2 of 9**

What is the protagonist's primary source of power? In thrillers, "power" means the skills, resources, and access that allow the protagonist to fight back. What is their core capability?

A) Intelligence and information networks
   The protagonist's power is what they know and who they know. They have
   sources, contacts, access to databases, and the analytical ability to
   connect disparate pieces of information. Their network IS their weapon.

B) Political connections
   The protagonist has relationships with people in power—elected
   officials, appointees, senior bureaucrats, media figures. They can
   make phone calls that open doors, apply pressure through channels,
   and navigate institutional politics.

C) Technical expertise
   The protagonist has deep knowledge in a specific technical domain—
   cybersecurity, forensic accounting, medicine, engineering, law. Their
   expertise allows them to see things others miss and to operate in
   environments where generalists would be lost.

D) Financial resources
   The protagonist has access to money—their own, their organization's,
   or funds they can redirect. Money buys time, equipment, travel, legal
   representation, and silence. But it also creates a trail.

E) Media access and manipulation
   The protagonist can shape public narrative through media contacts,
   social media, press conferences, or leaked documents. Information
   is their weapon, and the court of public opinion is their battlefield.

F) Combat and tactical training
   The protagonist has military, law enforcement, or intelligence
   training that gives them physical capabilities—surveillance,
   counter-surveillance, weapons proficiency, tactical planning,
   escape and evasion.

G) Other (describe)
```

### Q9.5 — Key Locations

```
**WORLD FORGE — Question 5 of 9**

Which locations do you need? Thriller locations are defined by their FUNCTION in the power dynamic. Each location should serve the tension. Select 4-5.

A) The safe house
   A place where the protagonist can think, plan, and breathe. It is
   temporary by nature—safe houses get burned. The sense of security
   it provides should be fragile and precious.

B) The surveillance post
   A location from which the protagonist watches, listens, or monitors.
   It represents their information-gathering capability. It is usually
   cramped, uncomfortable, and isolating.

C) The power center
   The office, boardroom, chamber, or headquarters where the antagonist
   operates. It represents institutional authority. The protagonist may
   need to enter it for a meeting, a confrontation, or an infiltration.

D) The dead drop location
   A public or semi-public place used for covert exchanges. It should be
   ordinary enough to be unremarkable but specific enough to be evocative.
   A park bench, a library shelf, a coffee shop, a locker.

E) The interrogation room
   A place where information is extracted through pressure. This may be
   a formal facility or an improvised location. The power dynamic in
   this room should be palpable and potentially reversible.

F) The escape route
   A border crossing, a harbor, an airfield, a tunnel—the path OUT.
   It represents the protagonist's last resort and their connection to
   safety. It should be established early and threatened later.

G) The public space where private things happen
   A crowded restaurant, a busy street, a conference, a party. A location
   where the protagonist must conduct dangerous business while maintaining
   a facade of normalcy. The contrast between the public setting and the
   private stakes creates tension.

H) Other (describe)
```

## Document 7: Constellation Bible — Protagonist Profile

### Protagonist Overrides

```
**CONSTELLATION BIBLE — Protagonist**

**The Unlit State:** Who are they before Ignition?
A) A professional doing their job competently but without passion. They follow
   the rules, file the reports, and go home. Something is missing but they
   cannot name it.
B) A retired or former operative trying to live a normal life. They left
   for a reason—burnout, moral crisis, personal loss—and they do not want
   to go back.
C) A civilian with specialized knowledge who has no idea their expertise
   makes them a target. They are living an ordinary life that is about to
   become impossible.
D) An insider who has been quietly noticing problems—irregularities in the
   data, inconsistencies in the reports, behavior that does not add up. They
   have not acted yet.
E) Other (describe)

**The Wound:** What haunts them from before the story?
A) A case they failed—someone died, someone got away, justice was not served.
   The specifics may be classified but the guilt is not.
B) A betrayal by someone they trusted with their life—a partner, a mentor,
   a handler, a spouse. The betrayal shattered their ability to trust.
C) Something they did in the line of duty that they cannot justify. An order
   followed, a choice made, collateral damage inflicted. They were told it
   was necessary. They are not sure.
D) A personal loss connected to their professional life—a family member
   targeted because of their work, a relationship destroyed by secrecy,
   a child they cannot protect.
E) An institutional betrayal—they reported wrongdoing and were punished for
   it. Or they discovered that the organization they served was not what
   they believed.
F) Other (describe)

**The Lie:** What do they believe about themselves that is false?
A) "I can handle this alone." They believe asking for help is weakness, that
   trust is a vulnerability they cannot afford.
B) "The rules will protect me." They believe that following procedure, working
   within the system, and doing things the right way will be enough.
C) "I have left that life behind." They believe they can walk away from who
   they were and what they know. They cannot.
D) "I am not the kind of person who does that." They believe they have moral
   limits that are absolute. They are about to discover those limits are
   more flexible than they thought.
E) "If I find the truth, everything will be fixed." They believe that
   exposure alone is sufficient—that revealing the conspiracy will solve
   the problem. The truth is more complicated.
F) Other (describe)
```

## Document 8: Mercy Ledger — Thriller Overrides

### Mercy Recipients

```
**MERCY [X] — Question 1 of 4**

Who receives this mercy? In thrillers, mercy is extended to people who are
dangerous, compromised, or morally ambiguous. The act of mercy should feel
risky and potentially foolish.

A) An enemy operative who is captured or cornered
   Someone working for the antagonist who is now at the protagonist's mercy.
   They may have information. They may be following orders they do not agree
   with. They may be lying about everything.

B) A compromised colleague who was coerced into betrayal
   Someone on the protagonist's side who was turned—through blackmail,
   threats to family, or ideological persuasion. They have done real damage,
   but they may not be irredeemable.

C) A civilian caught in the crossfire
   An innocent person who has seen too much, who is in the wrong place, or
   who is being used as leverage. Protecting them costs time and resources
   the protagonist cannot spare.

D) A low-level conspirator who wants out
   A functionary, a courier, a technician—someone deep enough in the
   conspiracy to have useful information but low enough to be expendable.
   They are scared and looking for a way out.

E) The antagonist's family member or loved one
   Someone connected to the antagonist by blood or bond who is innocent of
   the conspiracy. Using them as leverage would be effective. Protecting
   them instead is a choice with consequences.

F) Other (describe)
```

## Additional Override Notes

### Questions That Do NOT Need Overrides

The following questions work as-is across genres and should use their default options:

- **Q1.3 (Core Constellation Bond)** — Romantic, familial, friendship, mentor, and unlikely alliance bonds all work naturally in thrillers.
- **Q1.5 (The Forging Question)** — The options about what the protagonist must become are universal.
- **Q1.6 (Antagonist's Truth)** — These philosophical positions work across genres.
- **Q1.7 (The Surrender)** — Surrender options are genre-neutral.
- **Q1.8 (Theme)** — Thematic statements are universal.
- **Q1.9 (Blade's Purpose)** — Post-story roles work across genres.
- **Q2.1 (Burden's Origin)** — Discovery methods are genre-neutral.
- **Q2.2 (Why This Protagonist)** — These reasons work in any genre.
- **Q3.1-Q3.7 (Fire Strand)** — Fire mechanics are internal and genre-neutral.
- **Q4.1-Q4.3, Q4.5-Q4.6 (Constellation)** — Relationship dynamics are universal.
- **Q5.x (Forge Points)** — Forge Point structure is genre-neutral; the genre rules guide content.
- **Q6.1-Q6.3, Q6.5-Q6.9 (Dark Mirror)** — Most Dark Mirror questions work across genres.
- **Q9.3-Q9.4 (Power Limitations, Previous Wielders)** — Adapt naturally to thriller context.
- **Q9.6-Q9.8 (World-Protagonist Mirror, Cultures, History)** — Work with reinterpretation.

### Adaptation Guidance for Non-Overridden Questions

When presenting default questions in a thriller context, the planner should:

1. **Reframe fantasy language.** "Magic" becomes "capability." "Power" stays "power." "Prophecy" becomes "intelligence assessment" or "predicted outcome."
2. **Ground in institutional reality.** Kingdoms become agencies. Courts become boardrooms. Armies become task forces.
3. **Maintain the structural function.** The question is asking about the same narrative element regardless of genre. A "magical ability that corrupts with use" and "obsession with truth that costs relationships" serve the same structural purpose in the Fire Strand.
