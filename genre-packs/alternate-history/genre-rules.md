# Genre Rules: Alternate History

Alternate history is the literature of "what if this had gone differently?" -- taking a specific, identifiable moment in real history and tracing the consequences of a different outcome. In the Crucible framework, the three strands map powerfully: the Quest often involves uncovering, preserving, or confronting the consequences of the divergence; the Fire represents knowledge, perception, or connection to the "true" timeline that sets the protagonist apart and threatens to consume them; and the Constellation tests loyalty and trust in a world whose very foundation is different from what it "should" have been.

The genre demands two competencies from the author: deep knowledge of real history (the foundation you are diverging from) and rigorous extrapolation (the discipline to trace consequences honestly rather than conveniently).

---

## Genre Expectations

### A Clear, Specific Point of Divergence

Every alternate history must have a Point of Divergence (POD) -- a single, identifiable moment where the fictional timeline splits from the real one. The POD is not a vague "things went differently." It is a specific event, decision, battle, assassination, invention, or accident that occurred differently than it did in our world.

**Strong PODs:**
- "What if the Spanish Armada had succeeded in 1588?"
- "What if Lincoln had survived the assassination?"
- "What if the printing press had been invented in Song Dynasty China rather than Gutenberg's Germany?"
- "What if the Black Death had killed 90% of Europe's population rather than 30-50%?"

**Weak PODs:**
- "What if things were just different?" (No specificity)
- "What if magic existed?" (That is fantasy, not alternate history)
- "What if aliens intervened?" (That is science fiction with a historical setting)

The POD can be recent or ancient, large or small, but it must be traceable. The reader must be able to point to the moment where the timelines diverged.

**Crucible application:** The POD often connects directly to the World's Wound (Q9.1). The divergence created the conditions that produced the story's central conflict.

### Rigorous Extrapolation of Consequences

The difference between alternate history and historical fantasy is rigor. Alternate history demands that the author trace the consequences of the POD honestly -- not just the first-order effects ("the other side won the battle") but the second-, third-, and fourth-order effects that cascade through decades and centuries.

**Trace the ripples:**
- If the POD changes a war's outcome, who controls what territory? How does that reshape borders, trade routes, alliances, and enmities for the next century?
- If the POD changes a technological development, what was invented sooner? What was never invented? What was invented differently? How do those changes reshape daily life?
- If the POD changes a political system, who holds power? How do they maintain it? What resistance emerges? How do institutions evolve differently?

**The honesty test:** If your alternate world conveniently produces the same modern amenities as the real world despite a medieval-era divergence, you have not extrapolated honestly. The world should feel genuinely different -- sometimes uncomfortably so.

### Real History as Foundation

The reader must trust that the author knows the real history they are diverging from. Errors in the baseline history undermine the entire project. If you get the real Battle of Gettysburg wrong, the reader will not trust your alternate Battle of Gettysburg.

- Research the era thoroughly before diverging from it.
- Know the key figures, political dynamics, technologies, social structures, and cultural attitudes of the period.
- Understand why things happened the way they did in real history -- only then can you credibly change them.

### The "What If" Explored Thoroughly

Alternate history is not a premise; it is an exploration. The POD is the question. The story is the answer -- or rather, the sustained, thorough, unflinching examination of what that answer looks like for real people living through it.

- Do not set up an interesting divergence and then tell a story that could happen in any setting. The alternate history must be load-bearing -- remove it, and the story collapses.
- Explore how the divergence affects every level of society: the powerful and the powerless, the winners and the losers, the centers of power and the forgotten margins.
- The best alternate histories make the reader see their own world differently by showing what it could have been.

### Historical Figures Reimagined Consistently

When real historical figures appear in your alternate timeline, they must remain recognizably themselves while responding believably to different circumstances. Napoleon is still brilliant and ambitious even if he never became Emperor. Lincoln is still thoughtful and persuasive even if the Civil War ended differently. The person's core character traits should survive the divergence; their circumstances and choices should change.

- Do not reduce historical figures to caricatures or chess pieces.
- Consider how a different world would have shaped them differently. A Lincoln who grew up in a world where slavery ended peacefully would be a different man -- but still recognizably Lincoln.
- If a historical figure was never born (because the divergence occurred before their birth), note that honestly. Butterflies matter.

## Prose Guidelines

### Period-Appropriate Language and Register

The prose should feel rooted in the era of the story -- not a modern novel wearing a period costume. This does not mean imitating archaic prose styles in a way that becomes unreadable. It means calibrating vocabulary, sentence rhythm, and register to evoke the era.

**Guidelines:**
- Study how people of the era actually wrote and spoke. Read letters, diaries, newspapers, and speeches from the period.
- Adopt the era's register without sacrificing readability. A story set in 1860s America should not read like a 2024 blog post, but it also should not read like a pastiche of Victorian prose.
- Let the altered history affect the language itself. If French is the dominant world language because Napoleon won, characters might use French loan words where English ones exist in our timeline.
- Class, education, and region should affect speech and internal monologue. A dock worker and a diplomat speak differently in any era.

### Blending the Familiar and the Unfamiliar

The art of alternate history prose is making the reader simultaneously recognize and not recognize the world. The reader should constantly experience the tension between "I know this place" and "something is wrong here."

**Techniques:**
1. **Anchor first, then diverge.** Begin scenes with recognizable details -- a city name, a landmark, a cultural reference -- then introduce the differences. "The train pulled into Philadelphia's Broad Street Station, past the bronze statue of King George IV that had stood in the plaza since the Reconciliation."
2. **Casual divergence.** Characters do not remark on what is normal to them. They do not explain why there is a portrait of Emperor Norton in the post office. They glance at it and move on. The reader's confusion is productive.
3. **Familiar objects, unfamiliar contexts.** A telephone that exists in 1850 because of an earlier industrial revolution. A samurai sword on a Wall Street desk because Japan colonized the American west coast. The juxtaposition does the work.

### Grounding Before Diverging

Every scene should establish recognizable reality before introducing the alternate elements. The reader needs solid ground to stand on before you pull the rug.

- In early chapters, lean into familiar historical details to build trust.
- Introduce divergences gradually, letting the reader accumulate understanding.
- The further your world has diverged from reality, the more grounding detail you need per page.

### Avoiding Anachronisms in the Wrong Direction

Alternate history has a unique anachronism problem. In standard historical fiction, you avoid putting modern things in the past. In alternate history, you must also avoid putting "our" historical developments into a timeline where they would not have occurred. If the Industrial Revolution never happened, characters should not have factory-made goods. If Rome never fell, medieval European culture as we know it would not exist.

- Track your technology tree carefully. Changes cascade.
- When in doubt, err on the side of less modern, not more.
- Technology that seems "too advanced" for the era should be explicitly connected to the divergence.

## Dialogue Rules

### Period-Appropriate Speech

Characters should speak in a way that feels native to their era and their version of it. This is the single most immersive (or most immersion-breaking) element of alternate history.

**Guidelines:**
- Study actual speech patterns of the era through letters, court transcripts, and recorded interviews (for more recent periods).
- Avoid modern colloquialisms in pre-modern settings. No one in an 1840s alternate history says "okay" (the word barely existed) or "I'm good" (a 21st-century construction).
- Equally, do not over-archaize. People in 1860 did not speak like people in 1460. Get the right era, not just "old-timey."

### Class and Regional Dialects

Social class and geographic origin should be audible in dialogue -- and the alternate history may have changed which regions carry prestige and which carry stigma.

- If the South won the Civil War, Southern speech patterns might carry the authority that received pronunciation carries in Britain.
- If China colonized the Americas, the prestige dialect might blend Mandarin and local languages in ways English and French do in our world.
- Working-class characters should sound like working-class characters of that era. Aristocrats should sound like aristocrats. But the definitions of those classes may have shifted.

### How Changed History Affects Idioms and Expressions

Language is shaped by history. If history changes, language changes with it.

- Idioms rooted in specific events should be different. If Waterloo never happened, no one "meets their Waterloo." If the Titanic was never built, nothing is "unsinkable."
- New idioms should emerge from the alternate history's defining events. If the Great Pacific Earthquake of 1890 is this world's signature disaster, people might say "shaky as San Francisco" the way we say "unsinkable."
- Religious and cultural references shift. If the Reformation never happened, Protestant idioms do not exist. If Islam became dominant in Western Europe, everyday expressions reflect that.
- Do not overload the dialogue with invented idioms. A few, deployed naturally, create the sense of a living alternate language. Too many become a puzzle.

### Characters Referencing "Their" History Naturally

This is the most important dialogue rule in alternate history: characters must speak about their world's history as naturally as we speak about ours. They do not explain their history to each other. They reference it in passing, allude to it, argue about it, take it for granted.

- A character in a world where the British Empire still controls North America might say, "Not since the Troubles of '76," and every character in the room knows what she means. The reader must figure it out.
- History classes, monuments, holidays, and cultural touchstones are different. Let characters reference these things without footnotes.
- When characters do discuss history, they should disagree about it the way real people do. History is contested, not settled.

## Divergence System Rules

### Sanderson's Laws Adapted for Alternate History

Brandon Sanderson's laws of magic apply directly to the divergence that defines an alternate history world. The divergence is the engine of the story, and like any engine, it needs rules.

**First Law -- Resolution Proportional to Understanding:**
The degree to which the divergence can satisfyingly drive conflict resolution is proportional to how well the reader understands that divergence. If the climax depends on a consequence of the POD, the reader must have encountered that consequence earlier in the story.

- The Apex Forge Point must not reveal a previously unknown consequence of the divergence to solve a problem. That is deus ex machina with a history textbook.
- Seed consequences early. Let the reader build their understanding of this world's rules. When those rules produce the climactic crisis, it should feel inevitable.

**Second Law -- Limitations Greater Than Novelties:**
What the alternate world CANNOT do or does NOT have matters more than what it can or does. The absences, the things that were never invented, the freedoms that were never won, the alliances that were never formed -- these limitations create the story's tension.

- If the American Revolution never happened, the concept of democratic revolution may be suppressed or delayed worldwide. That absence shapes every political conflict in your story.
- If the internal combustion engine was never developed, the absence of automobiles, trucks, and aircraft reshapes warfare, cities, trade, and daily life.
- Readers engage with what is missing because it creates productive disorientation.

**Third Law -- Explore One Divergence Deeply Before Adding Another:**
Before introducing a second POD (or a supernatural element, or time travel), fully explore the consequences of the first. Multiple divergences dilute each other and make the world feel arbitrary rather than rigorously constructed.

- One well-explored divergence creates a deep, believable world. Three under-explored divergences create a shallow, confusing one.
- If you must have multiple divergences, make them causally connected. The second divergence should be a consequence of the first, not an independent event.
- Exception: secret history, where the "second divergence" is the revelation that the first was engineered. This works because it deepens rather than dilutes.

### Divergence Consistency Checklist

For your Point of Divergence, answer:

1. **What exactly changed?** (Specific event, decision, or outcome)
2. **When did it change?** (Precise date or period)
3. **What are the first-order consequences?** (Immediate effects within 1-10 years)
4. **What are the second-order consequences?** (Effects within 10-50 years)
5. **What are the third-order consequences?** (Effects within 50-200 years)
6. **What stayed the same?** (Historical inertia -- what resists change?)
7. **What was never invented, discovered, or achieved?** (The absences)
8. **How do people in this world understand their own history?** (What is taught, suppressed, or mythologized?)

## Tropes: Use Well vs. Avoid

### Use Well

- **The ordinary person in extraordinary history.** Alternate history shines when it follows someone who is not a king, general, or president but who experiences the altered world from ground level. The Crucible's emphasis on personal transformation maps perfectly -- a shopkeeper, a teacher, a soldier who is forced to confront the divergence and its consequences.
- **Historical figures in unfamiliar roles.** A Lincoln who became a frontier lawyer and never entered politics. A Napoleon who became a brilliant artillery officer but never seized power. Familiar people in unfamiliar circumstances create powerful reader engagement.
- **The weight of "what should have been."** Characters who somehow know (or suspect) that the world is "wrong" carry enormous dramatic weight. This maps directly to the Fire strand -- knowledge of the true timeline as a power that threatens to consume the protagonist.
- **Parallel lives.** Characters whose counterparts in our timeline made different choices, achieved different things, or met different fates. The Dark Mirror can embody the "road not taken" on a personal level.
- **Cascading consequences.** Small changes producing large effects over time. A treaty signed one day earlier. A ship that arrived in time. A bullet that missed. The pleasure of alternate history is watching the dominoes fall differently.
- **Living under the victors' narrative.** Characters who exist within a historical narrative they know is constructed. The winners wrote the history books, and the protagonists live inside those books. This creates natural Crucible tension between compliance and resistance.

### Avoid or Subvert

- **"Nazi victory" as default.** The "what if Germany won WWII" scenario has been done extensively and well (by Philip K. Dick, Philip Roth, and others). If you use it, you must bring a genuinely new angle. The genre has more to offer than this single divergence.
- **"Great man" theory uncritically applied.** The assumption that history pivots entirely on the decisions of individual leaders. Real history is shaped by economics, demographics, technology, geography, disease, and culture as much as by individuals. If your POD is "one person did something different," trace the structural consequences, not just the personal ones.
- **Butterfly effect as hand-wave.** "A butterfly flapped its wings, so everything is different" is not extrapolation; it is an excuse to build whatever world you want without doing the work. Historical inertia is real. Some things resist change. The discipline of alternate history is knowing which things change and which persist.
- **Modern morality imposed on historical figures.** Characters from 1850 should not think like people from 2024, even in an altered timeline. They may be more progressive or more regressive than their real-world counterparts depending on the divergence, but they should think in the moral frameworks of their era and circumstances.
- **Secret history that explains everything.** "The Illuminati have been controlling history all along" can work as a premise, but it becomes lazy when it eliminates the need for genuine historical extrapolation. If a secret cabal controls everything, you are writing conspiracy fiction, not alternate history.
- **Time traveler as tourist.** A time traveler who simply observes the alternate world without being changed by it wastes the Crucible's transformative structure. If a time traveler is present, they must be a full Crucible protagonist -- transformed, broken, and forged by the experience.
- **Reset-button endings.** If the climax involves "fixing" history so it returns to our timeline, the story has not earned its Crucible. The protagonist and the world must be permanently changed. The altered timeline must be taken seriously as a real place with real people, not a puzzle to be solved.

## Subgenre Notes

### Military Alternate History
- The POD is typically a battle, campaign, or strategic decision. The story explores how a different military outcome reshapes geopolitics.
- Combat must feel accurate to the era's technology -- or to the divergent technology tree if the POD changed military development.
- The Crucible's Hardening phase (Movement III) maps naturally to soldiers forced to fight for a cause they increasingly question in a war that "should not" have happened.
- Do not glorify war. Show its costs: physical, psychological, moral. The Mercy Engine is powerful in military contexts -- mercy shown to an enemy soldier from a nation that "should not" exist.

### Political Alternate History
- The POD reshapes governance, ideology, or political movements. The story explores power structures that emerged differently.
- Political intrigue drives the Quest strand. The protagonist navigates institutions that operate on rules the reader must learn alongside them.
- The Dark Mirror is often a political figure who embodies the divergence's worst consequences -- someone who rose to power because of the altered history and will do anything to maintain it.
- The Constellation includes political allies whose loyalty is always conditional and whose interests never perfectly align.

### Secret History
- The divergence is hidden. The world appears to match our own, but beneath the surface, history went differently and someone has been concealing the truth.
- This subgenre leans heavily on the Quest strand -- uncovering the secret IS the quest.
- The Fire strand might involve the protagonist's growing ability to perceive the hidden history, at the cost of their ability to function in the "official" world.
- The danger is that secret history can feel like conspiracy theory if not handled carefully. Ground the secret in specific, verifiable historical gaps or ambiguities.

### Alternate History with Supernatural Elements
- Magic, monsters, or unexplained phenomena exist alongside the historical divergence. The POD may be the supernatural element itself ("What if the Black Death was actually a vampire plague?").
- Be clear about whether the supernatural IS the divergence or exists alongside a historical divergence. Mixing the two without clarity creates confusion.
- Apply the same rigor to the supernatural element as to the historical divergence. Trace its consequences. If vampires exist, how has that shaped religion, medicine, warfare, politics, and daily life?
- The Fire strand can be supernatural in nature while the Quest strand remains historically grounded, creating productive tension between the two.

### Steampunk / Dieselpunk as Alternate History
- These aesthetics are alternate histories of technology: what if steam power (or diesel/Art Deco-era technology) advanced further than it did, or replaced the technologies that superseded it?
- The strongest steampunk/dieselpunk grounds its aesthetic in a specific POD rather than treating it as pure style. WHY does this world still run on steam? What happened (or did not happen) to prevent the transition to oil, electricity, or digital technology?
- The Fire strand can involve experimental technology that pushes the aesthetic's boundaries -- a steam-powered device that approaches the impossible, at escalating cost.
- Avoid pure aesthetic without substance. A story is not alternate history because it has airships and goggles. It is alternate history because it asks "what if the world developed this way?" and takes the question seriously.

### Counterfactual Memoir
- The story is told as a memoir, autobiography, or personal account from within the alternate timeline. The narrator does not know (or barely suspects) that the world is different from what it "should" be.
- This form creates maximum immersion because the narrator treats the alternate world as simply "the world." Divergences are mentioned casually, without explanation.
- The reader must work to reconstruct the POD and its consequences from scattered references. This is demanding but deeply rewarding.
- The Crucible's emotional arc drives the narrative. The protagonist is not investigating the divergence; they are living their life in a world shaped by it. The stakes are personal, not cosmological.
- Voice is everything. The memoir form requires a distinctive, consistent narrative voice that belongs to the era and the altered world.
