# Genre Rules: Science Fiction

Science fiction is the literature of "what if?" — extrapolating from known science, technology, or social structures to explore how change transforms the human condition. In the Crucible framework, the three strands map naturally: the Quest often involves a technological or scientific objective, the Fire represents a power rooted in technology or augmentation rather than magic, and the Constellation tests human connection against the pressures of an altered world.

---

## Genre Expectations

### Scientific Plausibility or Internal Consistency

Not all sci-fi must be scientifically rigorous, but all sci-fi must be internally consistent. Choose your level of plausibility early and honor it throughout:

- **Hard SF:** Technology and science operate within known physical laws or plausible extensions. If you break a law of physics, break only one and explore the consequences thoroughly.
- **Soft SF:** Scientific accuracy takes a back seat to social, psychological, or philosophical exploration. The technology serves as a catalyst for human drama. Internal consistency still matters — your FTL drive does not need to be physically possible, but it must work the same way every time.
- **Speculative SF:** The science may be entirely invented, but the logic of cause and effect must hold. If neural implants cause personality drift in Chapter 3, they cannot be consequence-free in Chapter 12.

**Crucible application:** The Fire strand benefits enormously from internal consistency. If the protagonist's augmentation or technology exacts a cost, that cost must be predictable enough for readers to dread it.

### Technological Extrapolation

Good sci-fi asks: if this one thing changed, what else would change? Technology does not exist in isolation. Every significant invention reshapes economics, politics, relationships, warfare, art, and daily life.

- If you introduce FTL travel, consider what it does to colonialism, trade, communication, and political unity.
- If you introduce AI consciousness, consider legal rights, labor, companionship, warfare, and the definition of personhood.
- If you introduce genetic engineering, consider class divisions, beauty standards, disability, identity, and consent.

Follow the ripples. The best sci-fi worlds feel lived-in because the author traced the second- and third-order consequences of their core technologies.

### Social and Philosophical Commentary

Science fiction has always been a vehicle for examining the present through the lens of the future. The Crucible's theme statement (Document 1, Q1.8) should resonate with a real-world philosophical tension:

- Technology and identity (what makes us human?)
- Power and accountability (who controls transformative technology?)
- Progress and cost (what do we sacrifice for advancement?)
- Connection and isolation (does technology bring us together or drive us apart?)
- Freedom and security (what are we willing to surrender for safety?)

The theme does not need to be stated explicitly. It should emerge from the choices the protagonist makes and the consequences they face.

### Sense of Wonder

Science fiction readers come for the wonder — the vertigo of encountering something genuinely new. This can be:

- **Scale wonder:** The vast emptiness of space, the age of stars, the size of megastructures.
- **Conceptual wonder:** An idea that reframes how the reader thinks about reality.
- **Discovery wonder:** First contact, new worlds, technologies that change everything.
- **Human wonder:** The resilience, creativity, or compassion of people facing the impossible.

At least once per act, give the reader a moment that makes them stop and think: "I never considered that before."

## Prose Guidelines

### Technical Accuracy Without Info-Dumps

The cardinal sin of sci-fi prose is the lecture. Readers need to understand your technology, but they do not need a textbook chapter.

**Techniques for weaving in exposition:**

1. **Show the technology in use.** Instead of explaining how a neural interface works, show a character using one — and show what goes wrong.
2. **Let characters disagree about technology.** Two engineers arguing about a drive malfunction teaches the reader how the drive works without either character explaining something they both already know.
3. **Use consequence as exposition.** The reader learns what a plasma weapon does by seeing what it does to a bulkhead, not by reading its specifications.
4. **Introduce complexity gradually.** Establish the simple version first. Add complications when the plot demands them.
5. **Trust the reader.** You do not need to explain everything. A character checking their "cortical stack" implies a world of neural technology without requiring a paragraph of explanation.

**The Iceberg Rule:** Know ten times more about your technology than you put on the page. Readers sense depth. They do not need to see all of it.

### Balancing Exposition with Narrative

Every paragraph of exposition must earn its place by doing at least two jobs:

- Explain technology AND reveal character (she hated the implant's cold hum behind her ear)
- Describe a setting AND create tension (the station's failing gravity plating meant the ceiling could become the floor without warning)
- Convey history AND foreshadow conflict (the colony ships had carried enough genetic diversity for ten thousand — only three thousand arrived)

If a passage of exposition only explains, cut it or rework it.

### Handling Technobabble

Technobabble — invented jargon for invented technology — is a tool, not a crutch.

**When to use it:**
- To establish atmosphere and immersion (a mechanic cursing at a "misaligned phase coupler" feels real)
- To show character expertise or ignorance (who understands the jargon and who does not?)
- To create urgency ("The containment field is cycling — we have maybe six inversions before cascade failure")

**When to avoid it:**
- When it substitutes for actual explanation of something the reader needs to understand
- When it makes critical plot points opaque
- When every character speaks in it regardless of their background

**The Comprehension Test:** If you removed the technobabble and replaced it with [THING], would the reader still follow the scene? If yes, the technobabble is flavoring (good). If no, you need actual exposition.

## Dialogue Rules

### Natural Tech-Speak

Characters in a technological world use technology the way we use electricity — without constantly remarking on it. No one in the real world says, "I'll use my cellular phone device to transmit a voice message." They say, "I'll call you."

- Characters familiar with technology use shorthand, abbreviations, and casual references.
- Characters unfamiliar with technology ask questions the reader also has — use these characters as exposition vehicles.
- Specialists talk differently from generalists. An engineer says "resonance cascade." A pilot says "the ship's shaking itself apart."

### Avoiding Dated Slang

Sci-fi dialogue ages poorly when it tries too hard to sound futuristic or leans on contemporary slang that will date quickly.

**Avoid:**
- Invented slang that requires a glossary ("That's totally vac, spacer!")
- Current-day internet slang projected unchanged into the far future
- Excessive profanity substitutions ("frak," "gorram") unless integral to worldbuilding

**Prefer:**
- Language that sounds natural and slightly formal — it ages better
- Slang rooted in the world's technology and culture (miners on an asteroid might use geological terms as slang; spacers might use navigational metaphors)
- Sparse, purposeful invented language that readers can derive from context

### Character Voice Through Jargon Familiarity

How a character relates to technology reveals who they are:

- **The expert:** Uses jargon casually, explains reluctantly, notices what is wrong before anyone else
- **The outsider:** Asks questions, uses wrong terminology, provides reader-proxy perspective
- **The pragmatist:** Does not care how it works, only whether it works
- **The idealist:** Sees potential, speaks about what technology could become
- **The damaged:** Refers to technology through trauma (the veteran who flinches at the sound of a charging weapon)

In the Crucible framework, the protagonist's relationship with their Fire-technology should evolve through their dialogue. Early: fear or awe. Middle: growing confidence bordering on arrogance. Hardening: cold efficiency. Mastery: respect and restraint.

## Technology System Rules

### Sanderson's Laws Adapted for Technology

Brandon Sanderson's laws of magic systems apply directly to science fiction technology. Adapted:

**First Law — Resolution Proportional to Understanding:**
The degree to which technology can satisfyingly resolve conflict is proportional to how well the reader understands that technology. If a quantum computer saves the day, the reader must understand what the quantum computer can do BEFORE the climax.

- Forge Points (especially the Apex) must be resolved using technology the reader has seen used, limited, and failed earlier in the story.
- Never introduce a new capability at the moment it is needed. That is deus ex machina with circuit boards.

**Second Law — Limitations Greater Than Capabilities:**
Weaknesses, costs, and limitations make technology interesting. What your tech CANNOT do matters more than what it can.

- The Fire strand depends on this. If the protagonist's augmentation has no downside, there is no internal conflict.
- Every technological capability should have at least one hard limitation and one cost of use.

**Third Law — Expand Before Adding:**
Before inventing a new technology to solve a problem, explore new applications, consequences, and interactions of technology you have already established.

- This prevents technology sprawl — the tendency for sci-fi worlds to pile invention on invention until nothing feels grounded.
- Readers bond with systems they understand. Adding new tech costs reader investment.

### Technology Consistency Checklist

For each major technology in your world, answer:

1. **How does it work?** (You need a one-paragraph explanation even if the reader never sees it)
2. **Who has access?** (Scarcity drives conflict)
3. **What does it cost to use?** (Energy, resources, health, time)
4. **What can it NOT do?** (Limitations create plot)
5. **What are the unintended consequences?** (Side effects create texture)
6. **How has society adapted to it?** (Ripple effects create world)
7. **How can it fail?** (Failure modes create tension)

## Tropes: Use Well vs. Avoid

### Use Well

- **Competence-based protagonists.** Characters who earn their role through skill, intelligence, and determination rather than destiny or chosen-one status. The Crucible's emphasis on transformation through sacrifice supports this naturally.
- **Technology with consequences.** Every advance costs something. The Fire strand embodies this — power that transforms the wielder.
- **Found family crews.** The Constellation strand maps beautifully to a ship's crew or research team becoming a surrogate family. Earn the bonds through shared hardship.
- **First contact as mutual transformation.** Both sides are changed by the encounter. Neither is simply the "alien" to the other.
- **Corporate/institutional antagonists.** Bureaucracies and corporations as Dark Mirror figures — systems that started with good intentions and lost their way. More nuanced than pure evil empires.
- **AI as persons, not tools or threats.** AI characters with genuine interiority, desires, and moral complexity. They can be part of the Constellation or serve as the Dark Mirror.
- **Ethical dilemmas without easy answers.** The Crucible's sacrifice mechanics ensure that the protagonist cannot have everything. Technology amplifies these dilemmas.

### Avoid or Subvert

- **The chosen one.** If the protagonist is "the only one who can," make it because of their specific skills and choices, not prophecy or genetic destiny. Exception: if the story critically examines what "being chosen" does to a person.
- **AI goes evil.** The "AI decides to destroy humanity" plot has been done. If you use it, give the AI comprehensible reasoning that reflects a genuine philosophical position (Dark Mirror material).
- **Humans are special.** Avoid the trope where humans defeat technologically superior aliens through "heart" or "spirit." Win through cleverness, sacrifice, and adaptation — Crucible values.
- **Technology as purely good or evil.** Technology is a tool. The same neural interface that saves a life can erase a personality. Let the moral weight fall on the users, not the technology.
- **Monoculture aliens.** A species of billions should not share a single culture, language, and value system. Even partial alien perspectives benefit from internal diversity.
- **Reset-button endings.** If the climax involves resetting everything to the way it was, the story has not earned its Crucible. The protagonist and world must be permanently changed.
- **Women in refrigerators.** Do not kill, depower, or assault female characters solely to motivate male characters. The Constellation strand demands that every character death serve the story's thematic argument.

## Subgenre Notes

### Hard SF
- Research matters. Get the physics right or acknowledge where you are speculating.
- The wonder comes from the science itself. Let the ideas be beautiful.
- Characters can be brilliant without being emotionally stunted — avoid the "cold genius" cliche.
- The Crucible's Fire strand can be a hard-science phenomenon: a medical condition, a physics anomaly, an experimental procedure.

### Soft SF
- Focus on the human and social consequences of change.
- Technology exists to create conditions for character exploration.
- The Crucible's Constellation strand often dominates in soft SF — relationships under pressure from technological change.
- Ursula K. Le Guin, not Isaac Asimov, is your model.

### Cyberpunk
- The world is corporate-controlled, stratified, and surveilled. The protagonist operates in the cracks.
- Augmentation is common but unequally distributed. The Fire strand is often literal body modification.
- The Constellation strand involves trust in a world designed to prevent it.
- Avoid nihilism. The Crucible demands that connection and mercy matter — cyberpunk settings test this but should not negate it.

### Biopunk
- Biology replaces electronics as the primary technology.
- The Fire strand maps naturally to genetic modification, biological augmentation, or engineered evolution.
- Body horror is a tool, not a goal. Use it to explore identity, consent, and the boundaries of the self.
- Ecological consequences matter — engineered organisms do not stay contained.

### Solarpunk
- Optimistic, community-focused, ecologically conscious.
- Conflict comes from those who resist sustainable systems or exploit them for power.
- The Crucible's Mercy Engine is central — this subgenre rewards compassion and cooperation.
- The Dark Mirror might represent the temptation to impose "good" solutions by force.

### Post-Singularity
- Humanity has been fundamentally changed by AI, uploading, or technological transcendence.
- What does the Crucible's emphasis on sacrifice and surrender mean when death is optional?
- The Fire strand might involve transcendence itself — becoming something beyond human.
- Explore what is lost when limits are removed. The Crucible insists that limitations are necessary for growth.

### Near-Future (0-50 years)
- Technology is recognizable. Smartphones, social media, AI assistants, climate change.
- The mundane details must be accurate — readers will notice errors in familiar technology.
- The Fire strand can involve emerging technologies: CRISPR, neural interfaces, quantum computing.
- Political and social commentary is especially pointed because the world is recognizably ours.

### Far-Future (500+ years)
- Humanity may be unrecognizable. That is fine — the emotional core must still be human.
- World-building carries more weight. Spend time on how daily life works.
- The Constellation strand must establish WHY these characters care about each other in a world where the rules of human interaction may have changed.
- Scale is a tool. Use it to create wonder, not to make characters feel insignificant.

### Military SF
- Combat must feel real — physical consequences, psychological damage, moral compromise.
- The Crucible's Hardening phase (Movement III) maps naturally to the moral erosion of war.
- Chain of command creates natural Constellation dynamics — loyalty, duty, and the gap between orders and conscience.
- The Mercy Engine is especially powerful in military contexts. Mercy toward an enemy in wartime costs everything.

### First Contact
- Communication is the central challenge. Do not solve it too easily.
- Both sides should be comprehensible to the reader without being comprehensible to each other.
- The Constellation strand can include alien characters — but earn their alien-ness through consistent, non-human logic.
- First contact is a Forge Point by nature. Everything changes. Use it as one.
