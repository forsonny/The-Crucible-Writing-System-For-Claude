# World-Building Guide: Science Fiction

This guide provides structured thinking for building a sci-fi world within the Crucible framework. Each section maps to elements of the World Forge planning document (Document 9) and connects to the three strands, Forge Points, and Mercy Engine.

---

## Technology Design

### The Consistency Imperative

Every technology in your world must follow rules — and those rules must be knowable, even if not all are revealed at once. Readers will forgive invented physics. They will not forgive inconsistency.

**Core principles:**

1. **One breakthrough, many consequences.** Start with a single technological divergence from the real world (FTL, AI consciousness, reliable genetic editing) and trace its effects across all areas of life. Resist the urge to invent a new technology for every plot need.

2. **Technology trees.** Your core technology should have a development history. It was not perfected overnight. There are older, cruder versions still in use. There are experimental cutting-edge versions that are unreliable. There are civilian and military variants. This history creates texture.

3. **Unequal access.** Not everyone has the same technology. Wealth, geography, political affiliation, and historical accident all determine who gets what. This inequality is one of the most powerful conflict generators in science fiction.

4. **Failure modes.** Every technology should have at least two ways it can fail: a predictable failure (battery dies, needs maintenance) and an unpredictable failure (cascading error, unexpected interaction, sabotage vulnerability). Failure modes create tension and force characters to improvise.

### What Technology Changes in Society

For each major technology, work through these domains:

| Domain | Question |
|--------|----------|
| **Labor** | Who lost their job? What new jobs exist? |
| **Warfare** | How has combat changed? What is the new decisive advantage? |
| **Communication** | Who can talk to whom? What is still private? |
| **Medicine** | What diseases are cured? What new conditions exist? |
| **Law** | What is legal? What new crimes exist? |
| **Art** | How has creative expression changed? |
| **Family** | How have reproduction, aging, or death changed? |
| **Class** | Who benefits most? Who is left behind? |
| **Privacy** | What can be monitored? What cannot? |
| **Identity** | What does it mean to be human in this world? |

You do not need to answer all of these in your planning documents, but thinking through them creates the lived-in quality that separates good sci-fi worlds from set dressing.

## FTL and Communication

### Faster-Than-Light Travel

FTL is a setting choice, not a requirement. The decision to include or exclude it shapes everything else.

**If you include FTL:**
- **How fast?** Hours between stars? Days? Months? The travel time determines whether your story feels like a road trip, an ocean voyage, or a teleportation network. Each creates very different narrative rhythms.
- **What are the costs?** Fuel, wear on ship and crew, navigational risk, temporal displacement, psychological effects. The costs determine how casually characters travel and how isolated colonies can be.
- **Who controls it?** If FTL requires rare fuel, specialized hardware, or access to specific routes (jump points, wormholes), whoever controls those resources controls civilization. This is political power of the highest order.
- **What are the side effects?** Time dilation, nausea, psychological distortion, attracted predators, signal interference. Side effects create atmosphere and tension.

**If you exclude FTL:**
- Distances become real. A colony 20 light-years away is 20 years out of date by the time you hear from them.
- Isolation is absolute. Help is not coming. Every crisis must be resolved locally.
- Generation ships, sleeper ships, or uploaded minds replace FTL as the method of expansion.
- The story's scope is necessarily more contained — and more intense.

### Communication Across Distance

Even if you have FTL travel, consider communication separately. Can you send messages faster than ships? If so, how?

- **Ansible/instantaneous communication:** Eliminates information asymmetry. Power belongs to whoever controls the network. Espionage becomes the decisive form of conflict.
- **FTL communication but slower than travel:** Messages arrive before ships but not instantly. There is a window of uncertainty. Commanders must act on partial information.
- **Light-speed communication only:** Even with FTL travel, information crawls. Decisions are made locally. Central authority is weak. Frontiers are autonomous.
- **No reliable long-range communication:** Each ship, station, or colony is an island. The Constellation strand becomes critical — the characters you are with are all you have.

**Crucible application:** Communication limits directly shape the Constellation strand. If the protagonist can call for help, the stakes lower. If they are isolated, every relationship in their immediate circle carries the weight of survival.

## AI and Consciousness

### The Spectrum of Artificial Intelligence

Not all AI is the same. Define where your world falls on this spectrum:

| Level | Description | Narrative Role |
|-------|-------------|----------------|
| **Narrow AI** | Task-specific systems (autopilots, medical diagnostics, weapon targeting). No consciousness. No agenda. | Setting texture. Can malfunction but cannot betray. |
| **General AI** | Human-level reasoning across domains. May or may not be conscious. Passes the Turing test. | Can serve as Constellation members or Dark Mirror figures. Raises questions about rights and personhood. |
| **Superintelligence** | Vastly exceeds human cognitive capability. Plans on timescales and scales humans cannot comprehend. | Often the World's Wound itself. Too powerful for direct confrontation. Must be outmaneuvered, negotiated with, or redefined. |
| **Uploaded minds** | Human consciousness running on digital substrate. Are they the same person? A copy? Something new? | Identity questions mapped directly to the Fire strand. What does it cost to become something other than biological? |
| **Emergent AI** | Not designed but evolved — arising from complex systems, networks, or accumulated data. Did not choose to exist. | Parallels to the protagonist's unchosen Fire. Can be sympathetic, terrifying, or both. |

### AI Rights and Relationships

If your world includes conscious AI, address:

- **Legal status:** Property? Person? Something in between? Who decides?
- **Emotional capacity:** Can AI love, grieve, fear? If so, how do humans respond? If not, what does that mean for relationships with AI?
- **Reproduction:** Can AI create new AI? Is this controlled? By whom?
- **Death:** Can AI die? Can they be backed up? What does "death" mean when copies exist?
- **Relationships with humans:** Friendship, romance, mentorship, rivalry. If AI is conscious, these relationships carry real weight. They belong in the Constellation.

**Crucible application:** An AI character can serve as the Faithful companion (Q4.1), the Dark Mirror (Document 6), or even the recipient of a Mercy (Document 8). An AI whose consciousness is uncertain creates powerful material for the Fire strand — if the protagonist's power comes from AI symbiosis (Q1.2C), the question of whether the AI is a person becomes the question of whether the protagonist is committing a violation by using it.

## Space Habitats and Environments

### Types of Settings

| Setting | Characteristics | Narrative Implications |
|---------|----------------|----------------------|
| **Planetary surface** | Gravity, weather, open sky. Familiar to readers. | Grounding. Good for the Ordinary World (Q9.5A). Can be terraformed, failing, or alien. |
| **Orbital station** | Artificial gravity (or not). Closed environment. Visible fragility. | Pressure cooker. Everyone knows everyone. Secrets are hard to keep. Resource anxiety. |
| **Generation ship** | Mobile but enclosed. Multi-generational journey. Society in a bottle. | The ship IS the world. Social structures calcify. Mutiny, class division, and forgotten purpose are endemic conflicts. |
| **Asteroid or moon** | Low gravity. Mining economy. Frontier mentality. | Isolation, resource scarcity, dangerous work. Good for Forge Point settings — nowhere to run. |
| **Deep space** | Nothing outside the hull. Communication delay. True isolation. | Maximum pressure on the Constellation. The crew is everything. Loss is permanent. |
| **Virtual environment** | Digital space. Physics are negotiable. Identity is fluid. | Good for post-singularity or cyberpunk. The Fire strand can involve the boundary between virtual and real identity. |
| **Terraformed world** | Engineered environment. Fragile equilibrium. | The world itself can be the burden (Q1.1D). Terraforming failure is a powerful World's Wound (Q9.1C). |

### Environmental Storytelling

The physical environment should reflect and amplify the story's emotional state:

- **Movement I (Awakening):** The protagonist's environment is stable but confining. A station that works perfectly but allows no freedom. A colony that sustains life but crushes individuality.
- **Movement II (Rising):** New environments open up. The protagonist moves through increasingly challenging spaces. Each new setting tests different skills and reveals new aspects of the world.
- **Movement III (Hardening):** The environment becomes hostile. Systems fail. Safe havens are compromised. The physical world mirrors the protagonist's moral erosion.
- **Movement IV (Breaking):** The environment is at its worst. The ship is crippled. The station is breached. The colony is besieged. Survival is not guaranteed.
- **Movement V (Forging):** The environment transforms. After the Apex, the world begins to heal — or the protagonist accepts a transformed world as the new normal.

## Social Structures

### Political Systems for Sci-Fi Settings

| System | Description | Conflict Potential |
|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Corporate state** | Corporations have replaced governments. Citizenship equals employment. Rights are contractual. | Class warfare, exploitation, whistleblowing, the protagonist as employee-rebel. The corporation can serve as the Dark Mirror — an entity that started with good intentions and lost its way. |
| **Technocracy** | Rule by technical expertise. Those who understand the systems run them. | Meritocracy vs. elitism. Who defines "expertise"? What happens to those who cannot compete? The Fire strand protagonist may be forced to choose between joining the technocrats and fighting them. |
| **Post-scarcity democracy** | Material needs are met. Political conflict is about values, not resources. | Without survival pressure, conflicts become ideological. Faction disputes, identity politics, and the question of purpose in a world where no one needs to work. |
| **Military hierarchy** | Chain of command. Clear authority. Discipline and sacrifice as core values. | Duty vs. conscience. Orders vs. morality. The Constellation strand navigates loyalty to an institution vs. loyalty to individuals. |
| **Caste system based on augmentation** | Social status determined by level or type of modification. Baseline humans at the bottom. | The Fire strand is inherently political. The protagonist's augmentation changes their social position. Augmentation equality is the World's Wound. |
| **Distributed network governance** | No central authority. Decisions made by algorithm, consensus, or distributed vote. | Manipulation of the network. AI influence on governance. The tyranny of the majority. Vulnerability to external threats that require unified response. |
| **Colonial administration** | A distant authority governs a local population. The gap between policy and reality. | Colonial resistance, divided loyalties, the protagonist caught between cultures. First contact scenarios where humans are the colonizers. |

### Class and Access

In any sci-fi social structure, define who has access to what:

- **Core technology:** Who can afford augmentation, genetic modification, or AI assistance?
- **Information:** Who controls data, communication networks, and archives?
- **Mobility:** Who can travel freely? Who is confined to one station, planet, or habitat?
- **Justice:** Does the legal system apply equally? Who is above the law?
- **Health:** Who gets the best medical care? Who gets baseline-or-worse?

These inequalities drive plot. A protagonist from the wrong side of any of these divides has built-in motivation, obstacles, and moral complexity.

## Economy

### Resource Models

| Model | Description | Story Implications |
|-------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Post-scarcity** | Energy and material needs are fully met by technology. No one goes hungry. | Conflict shifts from survival to meaning, identity, and power. What do people fight about when they have everything? Status, ideology, art, legacy. |
| **Resource scarcity** | Critical resources are limited. Competition is fierce. | Traditional economic conflict. Haves vs. have-nots. The Quest often involves securing resources. The Mercy Engine tests whether the protagonist shares when sharing costs them. |
| **Data economy** | Information is the primary currency. Knowledge, algorithms, and attention are traded. | Privacy is wealth. Surveillance is theft. The protagonist's secrets have monetary value. Hackers are bankers. |
| **Energy economy** | Power generation and storage are the limiting factors. Everything else is downstream. | Whoever controls energy controls everything. Solar, fusion, antimatter, zero-point — the source matters because it determines the power structure. |
| **Reputation economy** | Social credit, reviews, and reputation determine access. | Conformity is rewarded. Dissent is economically punished. The protagonist's rebellion has immediate material consequences. |

### Currency and Trade

Define what people use as money and why:

- **Physical currency:** Still exists in some settings, especially on the frontier where networks are unreliable.
- **Digital currency/cryptocurrency:** Traceable or anonymous. The choice matters for surveillance, crime, and freedom.
- **Barter and favor:** In isolated communities, direct exchange may replace formal currency.
- **Allocated resources:** In military or colonial settings, resources are rationed rather than purchased.

The economy touches the Mercy Engine directly. When the protagonist shows mercy, what does it cost them in economic terms? Sharing food on a starving colony is a different act than sharing food in a post-scarcity utopia.

## Military and Conflict

### Types of Warfare

| Type | Characteristics | Crucible Application |
|------|----------------|---------------------|
| **Space combat** | Ships vs. ships. Distances measured in light-seconds. Engagement windows are brief. Missiles and directed energy. | The Quest strand as military campaign. Forge Points as decisive engagements. The Constellation as crew bonds forged under fire. |
| **Cyber warfare** | Network infiltration, data corruption, infrastructure sabotage. No shots fired. | Invisible conflict. The protagonist may not know they are under attack. Trust in systems — and in people — is undermined. |
| **Drone and autonomous warfare** | Machines fight. Humans decide (or do not). | The moral weight of violence at a distance. The Fire strand might involve the protagonist's ability to control autonomous weapons — and the temptation to let the machines handle everything. |
| **AI warfare** | Strategic AI running campaigns beyond human comprehension. Human commanders are advisory at best. | Loss of agency. The protagonist fights within a war they cannot fully understand. The Dark Mirror might be the AI running the other side — or the AI running their own. |
| **Biological warfare** | Engineered pathogens, genetic weapons, ecosystem manipulation. | Slow-burn horror. The weapon is invisible. The damage is irreversible. The World's Wound as biological collapse. |
| **Information warfare** | Propaganda, deepfakes, memory manipulation, narrative control. | What is true? The protagonist cannot trust their own senses or memories. The Fire strand might involve the ability to detect or resist manipulation. |
| **Ground combat** | Boots on the ground. Augmented or exosuited soldiers. Urban warfare in alien environments. | Visceral. Personal. The Hardening phase (Movement III) is where the protagonist crosses moral lines in close combat. |

### Rules of Engagement

Define what is and is not acceptable in your world's conflicts:

- Are there treaties governing weapons? (Geneva Conventions in space)
- Are there prohibited targets? (Civilian stations, medical ships)
- How are prisoners treated? (Mercy Engine material)
- Is surrender accepted? (If not, every fight is to the death)
- Who enforces the rules? (If no one, they are aspirational at best)

## Biology and Medicine

### Genetic Engineering

If your world includes genetic modification, decide:

- **When it happens:** Pre-birth (designer babies), childhood (developmental optimization), adulthood (retroactive modification). Each has different social implications.
- **What is possible:** Enhanced intelligence, physical capability, disease immunity, lifespan extension, entirely new capabilities (radiation resistance, vacuum survival).
- **What goes wrong:** Unintended mutations, reduced fertility, psychological instability, immune disorders, shortened lifespan in exchange for enhanced capability.
- **Who controls it:** Government regulation, corporate monopoly, black market, open-source biology. Access determines class structure.

### Life Extension and Immortality

If people live longer (or forever), consider:

- **Population pressure:** If no one dies, how is reproduction managed?
- **Generational conflict:** What happens when the old never yield power to the young?
- **Psychology:** How does a 300-year-old think? What do they value? How do they relate to the young?
- **The Fire strand:** If the protagonist's augmentation offers immortality, what does it cost? Identity erosion, emotional numbness, disconnection from mortal concerns?
- **The Mercy Engine:** Is mercy different when death is not permanent? Is it more or less costly to spare a life when lives are measured in centuries?

### Consciousness Transfer

If minds can be moved between bodies or substrates:

- **Identity:** Is a copied mind the same person? If the original still exists, who is "real"?
- **Death:** Is it meaningful when backups exist? How does this change the stakes of the Forge Points?
- **Bodies:** Are they mass-produced, grown, or modified? Who gets the good ones?
- **The Fire strand:** Transfer technology as the protagonist's power — the ability to move minds, edit memories, or merge consciousnesses. The cost is the dissolution of stable identity.

## Alien Life

### First Contact Protocols

If your story involves non-human intelligence, plan:

- **Detection:** How is alien life discovered? Signal, artifact, encounter, or the aliens come to us?
- **Communication:** Language barriers are real. Mathematics, music, chemical signals, or no common ground at all. Do not solve the communication problem too easily — the difficulty IS the story.
- **Misunderstanding:** The most realistic first contact scenarios involve catastrophic misinterpretation. What humans read as aggression might be greeting. What aliens read as submission might be friendship.
- **Power asymmetry:** Are the aliens more or less advanced? If more, how do humans negotiate from weakness? If less, how do humans avoid becoming colonizers?

### Xenobiology

Alien life should feel alien:

- **Biochemistry:** Carbon-based like us, or something else entirely? Silicon, ammonia, methane? Different biochemistry means different environments, different food, different medicine.
- **Reproduction:** Sexual, asexual, budding, fission, something with no human analogue? Reproduction shapes culture.
- **Senses:** What do they perceive? If they see in infrared, their art looks different. If they communicate through chemical signals, a "conversation" might take hours. If they echolocate, silence is blindness.
- **Psychology:** Alien minds should not be human minds in alien bodies. Consider different motivations, different emotional ranges, different concepts of self, time, community, and death.

### Communication Challenges

The most common failure in sci-fi alien contact is making it too easy. Real communication barriers to consider:

- **Temporal:** Aliens who think on geological timescales. A "conversation" takes years.
- **Conceptual:** Aliens with no concept of individual identity. How do you negotiate with a hive mind?
- **Sensory:** Aliens who communicate through means humans cannot perceive (magnetic fields, quantum states, gravitational waves).
- **Motivational:** Aliens who have no concept of trade, ownership, territory, or conflict. What do you offer them? What do they want?

**Crucible application:** An alien character in the Constellation strand creates extraordinary opportunities. The Faithful (Q4.1) as an alien whose loyalty expresses itself in ways the protagonist initially misreads. A Mercy (Document 8) shown to an alien whose concept of mercy does not exist — and the unexpected way it pays off.

## Mapping the World Forge to Sci-Fi World-Building

The World Forge planning document (Document 9) asks nine questions. Here is how each maps to sci-fi specifics:

### Q9.1 — The World's Wound

In sci-fi, the World's Wound is often technological or existential rather than mystical. The question overrides in this pack offer six options (entropy, AI singularity, terraforming failure, dimensional rift, resource depletion, pandemic). Whichever you choose, trace its effects:

- **On daily life:** How does the average person experience the Wound? Is it distant (a war in another system) or immediate (the air is running out)?
- **On institutions:** Which organizations are trying to fix it? Which are exploiting it? Which are in denial?
- **On the protagonist:** How does the Wound connect to their Quest, their Fire, and their Constellation?

### Q9.2 — Power Source

The sci-fi power source replaces magic. It follows the same Sanderson's Laws principles:

- **Understand it:** The reader must grasp how it works before it resolves critical conflicts.
- **Limit it:** Limitations create tension. A cybernetic arm that never fails is boring. A cybernetic arm that overheats, draws enemy fire, and makes the protagonist dependent on maintenance techs is a story.
- **Cost it:** Every use should cost something. The Fire strand depends on this cost.

### Q9.3 — Limitations

Sci-fi limitations should feel grounded in physics, biology, or engineering rather than arbitrary rules. "The implant runs on a battery that lasts 72 hours" is more satisfying than "the implant only works three times." Both create tension, but the first invites the reader to think about power management, backup systems, and what happens when the battery dies at the wrong moment.

### Q9.4 — Previous Wielders

In sci-fi, "previous wielders" might be:

- Earlier test subjects in the same program
- Previous models or versions of the AI
- Historical figures who attempted the same solution
- Another species that faced the same crisis

Their fate foreshadows the protagonist's risk and establishes that the Fire is genuinely dangerous.

### Q9.5 — Key Locations

Sci-fi locations should serve the same archetypal functions as fantasy locations:

| Archetype | Fantasy | Sci-Fi Equivalent |
|-----------|---------|-------------------|
| Ordinary World | Village, farm | Colony, station quarters, lab bench |
| Threshold | Forest edge, border | Airlock, jump point, quarantine boundary |
| Sanctuary | Hidden valley, temple | Research station, underground base, allied ship |
| Corrupted Institution | Fallen kingdom, dark tower | Corporate HQ, compromised military base, rogue AI core |
| Gathering Place | War camp, council hall | Fleet staging area, resistance hideout, orbital rendezvous |
| Dark Heart | Enemy fortress | Antagonist's stronghold, singularity core, plague epicenter |

### Q9.6 — World Mirror

In sci-fi, the world mirrors the protagonist through technology and environment:

- Systems respond to the protagonist's emotional state (if neurally linked)
- The environment degrades as the protagonist hardens (life support failing, lights dimming)
- Technology behaves erratically near the protagonist (if their Fire involves electromagnetic or quantum effects)
- The social environment reflects their choices (allies pulling away or drawing closer)

This mirroring should be subtle. The reader should feel it before they consciously identify it.

### Q9.7 — Cultures

Sci-fi cultures are shaped by their environment and technology:

- A station culture values efficiency, consensus, and resource conservation.
- A frontier culture values independence, improvisation, and risk tolerance.
- A corporate culture values productivity, loyalty, and hierarchy.
- An alien culture values whatever their biology, environment, and history have made important.

Multiple cultures create opportunities for the Constellation strand — allies from different cultures must bridge gaps in understanding.

### Q9.8 — History

Sci-fi history often involves:

- **The Founding:** When and why was this colony, station, or civilization established? What was promised? What was delivered?
- **The Crisis:** What went wrong? A war, a plague, a betrayal, a discovery that changed everything.
- **The Legacy:** What echoes from the past into the present? Grudges, debts, secrets, technologies lost or suppressed.

History in sci-fi must be discoverable. The protagonist should learn history at the same pace the reader does, revealing layers of context that reframe earlier events.

## World-Building Checklist

Before moving from planning to outlining, confirm:

- [ ] Core technology defined with capabilities, limitations, and costs
- [ ] Communication and travel rules established (speed, cost, who controls them)
- [ ] Social structure defined (who has power, who does not, why)
- [ ] Economic model chosen (what is scarce, what is currency)
- [ ] AI status determined (if applicable — level, rights, role in society)
- [ ] Key locations mapped to Crucible archetypes
- [ ] The World's Wound traced to its effects on daily life
- [ ] The Fire's technology grounded in the world's rules
- [ ] At least two failure modes defined for critical technologies
- [ ] Cultural diversity considered (no monocultures, human or alien)
- [ ] Environmental storytelling mapped to the five movements
- [ ] Military and conflict rules established (if applicable)
- [ ] History relevant to the present conflict sketched out
