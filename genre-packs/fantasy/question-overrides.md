# Fantasy Genre — Question Overrides

Genre-specific answer options that REPLACE or AUGMENT the base question bank during planning. When the Fantasy genre pack is active, these options are presented instead of (or in addition to) the base options.

**How Overrides Work:**
- **REPLACE** means the base options are removed and only genre options appear
- **AUGMENT** means genre options are added alongside the base options
- Questions not listed here use the base options unchanged

---

## Document 1: Crucible Thesis

### Q1.1 — The Burden
**Override Type:** AUGMENT

**Additional Genre Options:**
- G) A magical artifact of immense power (ring, sword, crown, stone) that must be destroyed, delivered, or kept from the enemy
- H) A prophecy that must be fulfilled or broken
- I) A kingdom or realm that must be saved from collapse
- J) A magical seal, ward, or prison that must be maintained or restored

**Guidance:** Fantasy burdens often take physical form -- objects of power are the genre's signature. But the best fantasy burdens are also metaphorical. The Ring is not just a ring; it is the temptation of absolute power. Choose a burden whose physical nature reflects your theme.

---

### Q1.2 — The Fire
**Override Type:** REPLACE

**Genre Options:**
- A) A magical ability that corrupts with use -- Each casting takes something: memory, humanity, physical form. The more power wielded, the faster the transformation. The protagonist must use the very thing that is destroying them.
- B) A dark heritage or bloodline awakening -- The protagonist carries the blood of something terrible: a fallen god, an ancient tyrant, a cursed lineage. As their power grows, so does the pull of their ancestry. They are becoming what their bloodline demands.
- C) A transformation into something other than human -- The power is rewriting them. Physically, mentally, spiritually, they are becoming something else. Wings, scales, stone skin, or something subtler -- an alien way of thinking, a hunger that has no human name.
- D) A connection to a dangerous magical entity -- Something lives inside them or speaks through them. A bound demon, a dying god, a parasitic spirit, an elemental force. It grants power but has its own agenda, and the line between host and passenger is blurring.
- E) An addiction to magical power that costs their humanity -- The magic feels good. Too good. Each use is ecstasy, and the withdrawal is agony. The protagonist does not just wield power; they crave it. And the craving is rewriting who they are.
- F) A forbidden knowledge that warps perception -- They have seen or learned something mortals were not meant to know. Reality looks different now. They can do things others cannot, but they are losing the ability to see the world as others see it.
- G) Other (describe)

**Guidance:** In fantasy, the Fire Strand is almost always literal. The protagonist's internal transformation maps directly to a magical transformation. This is the genre's greatest strength for the Crucible Structure -- the Fire is visible, trackable, and terrifying.

---

### Q1.3 — Core Constellation Bond
**Override Type:** AUGMENT

**Additional Genre Options:**
- G) A magical bond (familiar, soul-bond, oath-forged connection)
- H) A feudal loyalty (liege-vassal, knight-squire)
- I) An order or guild bond (fellow mages, sworn brothers/sisters of a martial order)

**Guidance:** Fantasy allows bonds that are literally magical -- soul-bonds, familiar connections, oath-magic. These can be powerful because breaking them has supernatural consequences, not just emotional ones.

---

### Q1.4 — Dark Mirror Connection
**Override Type:** AUGMENT

**Additional Genre Options:**
- G) Same magical bloodline or power source, opposite philosophy of its use
- H) Former members of the same magical order or tradition
- I) Both touched by the same prophecy, claiming opposite roles

**Guidance:** The Dark Mirror in fantasy often shares the protagonist's magic, making them a direct preview of what the protagonist could become. This is enormously powerful because readers can SEE the transformation the protagonist risks.

---

### Q1.6 — Antagonist's Truth
**Override Type:** AUGMENT

**Additional Genre Options:**
- H) "Magic should not be hoarded by the few" -- democratization of power at any cost
- I) "The world was better in the Age of [X]" -- nostalgic imperialism dressed as restoration
- J) "Mortals cannot be trusted with free will" -- paternalistic tyranny born from genuine observation of mortal failure

**Guidance:** Fantasy antagonists often have truths rooted in magical philosophy or historical grievance. The best are those where the reader thinks, even briefly, "they have a point."

---

### Q1.7 — The Surrender
**Override Type:** AUGMENT

**Additional Genre Options:**
- H) Their magical gift itself (the Fire, permanently)
- I) Their immortality or extended lifespan
- J) Their place in the magical world (returning to ordinary mortality)

**Guidance:** The Apex Surrender in fantasy often involves releasing the very power that defined the protagonist throughout the story. This is thematically potent because it proves the story's thesis: identity is not power; character is not capability.

### Q2.4 — Quest Escalation
**Override Type:** REPLACE

**Genre Options:**
- A) Personal mission reveals ancient prophecy -- What began as a personal quest (revenge, rescue, survival) turns out to be the fulfillment of an ages-old foretelling. The protagonist is not just solving their problem; they are completing a pattern that has been waiting generations.
- B) Small threat is symptom of cosmic evil -- The local disturbance (a haunted forest, a corrupted well, a village of the vanished) is the surface expression of something vast. An ancient evil stirs, a seal weakens, a god turns its attention to the mortal world.
- C) Success or failure determines the fate of magic itself -- The outcome of the quest will determine whether magic persists, dies, transforms, or becomes something dangerous. Every magic user in the world has a stake in the result.
- D) Protagonist becomes the symbol a fractured alliance needs -- As word spreads of the protagonist's quest (and their Fire), factions that have been divided for ages begin to see a rallying point. The quest becomes bigger than one person because others make it so.
- E) The burden's true nature reveals world-threatening power -- What the protagonist carries or seeks is not what they thought. The artifact is a key to something worse. The knowledge unlocks a door that should stay closed. The person they protect is the vessel of something cosmic.
- F) Other (describe)

**Guidance:** Fantasy escalation should feel organic, not sudden. Plant seeds in Movement I that bloom in Movement III. The best escalation makes the reader re-read earlier scenes and think, "It was there all along."

---

### Q2.5 — Impossible Requirement
**Override Type:** AUGMENT

**Additional Genre Options:**
- H) The solution requires magic that has been lost for ages
- I) The quest demands entering a place where magic fails or behaves unpredictably
- J) The required sacrifice is a person the protagonist cannot bear to lose
- K) The enemy possesses the same power but has mastered it fully

**Guidance:** In fantasy, impossibility often involves magical barriers. The quest destination is warded, the required spell is lost, the enemy's fortress exists in a different plane. The resolution must come through character (the Mercy Engine), not through discovering a convenient counterspell.

## Document 4: Constellation Strand Map

### Q4.1 — Faithful Companion
**Override Type:** AUGMENT

**Additional Genre Options:**
- G) A magical creature bonded to the protagonist (familiar, mount, spirit companion)
- H) A fellow member of a magical order or tradition
- I) A non-magical companion whose ordinariness is their strength (the Sam Gamgee principle)

**Guidance:** The most powerful Faithful in fantasy is often the one WITHOUT magic. Their loyalty is purely human, uncomplicated by power dynamics. They carry the protagonist when magic fails -- literally embodying the Mercy Engine's principle that victory cannot come through power alone.

---

### Q4.2 — The Sacrifice
**Override Type:** AUGMENT

**Additional Genre Options:**
- G) A magical guardian whose sacrifice releases power the protagonist needs
- H) A character who absorbs a killing curse or magical attack meant for the protagonist
- I) A character who breaks a magical seal, knowing it will consume them

---

### Q4.4 — Constellation Expansion
**Override Type:** REPLACE

**Genre Options:**
- A) Magical practitioners rallied to the cause -- Mages, witches, druids, artificers, seers. They bring power but also politics, rivalries, and their own agendas. Each has their own relationship with the Fire the protagonist carries, and not all of them approve.
- B) Warrior orders with ancient oaths -- Knights, rangers, sellsword companies, or martial orders bound by honor codes older than living memory. Their strength is discipline and loyalty, but their codes may conflict with what the quest demands.
- C) Political figures with magical agendas -- Lords, queens, council members, guild masters. They have resources and influence, but every alliance comes with conditions. Their support is contingent, their loyalty calculated, and their agendas may diverge from the protagonist's at the worst possible moment.
- D) Common folk inspired to extraordinary courage -- Farmers, smiths, innkeepers, merchants who choose to stand despite having no magic, no training, and no obligation. Their presence proves the story's thesis: ordinary courage matters as much as extraordinary power.
- E) Former servants or allies of the Dark Mirror who defect -- Those who have seen the antagonist's true nature and rejected it. They bring inside knowledge but carry the suspicion of everyone else in the constellation. Trust is the price of their inclusion.
- F) Creatures or beings from the magical world -- Sentient non-humans, ancient entities, spirits of place, awakened beasts. Their perspectives are alien, their help is invaluable, and their motivations may be incomprehensible.
- G) Other (describe)

**Guidance:** The Constellation in fantasy should include at least one non-magical member and at least one member with a fundamentally different magical tradition or worldview. This diversity creates the relational friction that fuels Forge Points.

## Document 5: Forge Point Blueprints

### Forge Point Design — Fantasy Flavor

No question overrides for Document 5, but the following guidance applies when designing Forge Points in Fantasy:

**FP0 — Ignition:**
- The destruction of the ordinary world in fantasy is often literal: the village burns, the sanctuary falls, the mentor is killed by dark magic
- The Fire reveals itself for the first time, often in an uncontrolled burst that frightens the protagonist and those around them
- The magic system's cost should be established here -- even this first use should hurt

**FP1 — First Crucible:**
- The impossible choice often involves using dark or forbidden magic to save someone, at the cost of beginning the transformation
- A companion is typically alienated by the protagonist's power or choices related to it
- This is where "power without cost means nothing" becomes personal

**FP2 — Second Crucible:**
- Betrayal in fantasy often involves secrets about magical heritage, hidden agendas among magical allies, or the protagonist's own deceptions about their Fire
- The hardening phase may involve the protagonist crossing a magical moral line (using forbidden magic, taking a life with magic, breaking an oath)
- Political alliances fracture under the weight of magical revelation

**FP3 — Third Crucible:**
- The death at FP3 should involve magical sacrifice -- someone uses their last power, breaks a seal with their life force, or shields others from a killing magic
- Full power is required, meaning the protagonist must risk complete transformation/corruption
- The death should be someone whose magical knowledge or power cannot be replaced

**Apex — Willed Surrender:**
- The Apex in fantasy almost always involves the protagonist's relationship to magical power
- Surrendering the Fire itself (permanently losing magical ability) is the strongest form
- The surrender must be willed -- the protagonist must CHOOSE to give up magic when they could keep it

## Document 8: Mercy Ledger

### Mercy Recipients — Fantasy Archetypes
**Override Type:** AUGMENT

These recipient types are particularly effective in fantasy settings:

**Mercy 1 (The Seed):**
- A dark creature that shows unexpected sentience or suffering
- An enemy soldier ensorcelled or coerced into service
- A magical being trapped or bound against its will

**Mercy 2 (The Investment):**
- A former ally corrupted by magic who shows signs of fighting the corruption
- A rival magic user who lost control of their power and caused harm
- A political figure who betrayed the cause but repented when they saw the consequences

**Mercy 3 (The Risk):**
- An agent of the Dark Mirror who has begun to doubt
- A powerful magical creature that could be an ally or a catastrophe
- A character who previously betrayed the constellation but holds crucial knowledge

**Mercy 4 (The Impossible Gift):**
- The Dark Mirror themselves, or someone the Dark Mirror values
- A being responsible for the magical catastrophe that started the story
- Someone who killed a member of the constellation but is now defeated and helpless

## Document 9: World Forge

### Q9.1 — World's Wound
**Override Type:** REPLACE

**Genre Options:**
- A) Ancient evil awakening from long imprisonment -- Something sealed away in a previous age is breaking free. The wards are failing, the prison is cracking, and the echoes of its stirring are already warping the world. The protagonist's quest is connected to either re-sealing or destroying what emerges.
- B) Balance of magical forces disrupted -- The equilibrium that kept the world stable has been broken. Light and dark, creation and entropy, order and chaos -- whatever the opposing forces are, one is overwhelming the other. Weather goes wrong. Creatures act against their nature. Magic itself becomes unreliable.
- C) Source of magic failing or corrupted -- The wellspring, ley lines, world-tree, or cosmic mechanism that powers all magic is dying, poisoned, or being drained. Magic users grow weaker. Magical creatures sicken. The world is losing its wonder, and without intervention, magic will die entirely.
- D) World literally dying or unmaking -- The physical world is failing. Edges crumble into void. Time runs inconsistently. The laws of nature break down. This is not metaphorical; the world is ending, piece by piece, and the speed is increasing.
- E) Old order collapsing, new dark order rising -- The institutions that maintained magical balance (an order of mages, a council of elders, a compact between races) have fallen or been corrupted. In the vacuum, something worse is taking hold. The question is whether anything can be built from the ruins.
- F) Magical plague or corruption spreading across the land -- Something is spreading. It might be literal (a disease that transforms its victims) or metaphorical (a madness that turns magic users violent), but it is relentless and expanding. The protagonist may be immune, a carrier, or the cure.
- G) Other (describe)

**Guidance:** The World's Wound in fantasy should mirror the protagonist's Fire. If the protagonist's power corrupts them, the world should be suffering from a parallel corruption. If the protagonist must surrender their power at the Apex, the world should be suffering from an excess or abuse of power. The World Forge and the Fire Strand should resonate thematically.

---

### Q9.2 — Power Source
**Override Type:** REPLACE

**Genre Options:**
- A) Innate magical bloodlines -- Power is inherited, not learned. Some families, lineages, or species carry magic in their blood. This creates natural hierarchies, prejudices, and the question of whether power should be a birthright. The protagonist's Fire is often tied to a specific bloodline revelation.
- B) External forces channeled through the wielder -- Magic comes from outside: ley lines, celestial alignments, elemental forces, the raw stuff of creation. The wielder is a conduit, not a source. This means power can be blocked (cut off from the source), overwhelmed (too much flowing through), or corrupted (the source itself is tainted).
- C) Learned and studied arcane discipline -- Magic is a craft, mastered through years of study, practice, and intellectual rigor. Spellbooks, academies, master-apprentice traditions. This creates institutional magic with politics, hierarchies, and gatekeeping. Self-taught practitioners are viewed with suspicion.
- D) Divine or entity-granted power -- A god, spirit, demon, or cosmic being grants power in exchange for service, devotion, or sacrifice. The power comes with obligations, and the entity has its own agenda. The protagonist's Fire may involve the entity's influence growing stronger.
- E) Natural world connection -- Magic flows from the living world: trees, rivers, animals, stone, weather. Druids, shamans, green witches, beast-speakers. This magic is often gentle but vast, and disconnection from nature weakens or corrupts it. Urbanization and industry may be antagonistic forces.
- F) Artificed or crafted magic -- Magic is built into objects: enchanted weapons, runic constructions, alchemical compounds, clockwork automata. The magic user is an engineer of the supernatural. This creates questions of mass production, weaponization, and who controls the means of magical production.
- G) Emotional or willpower-based -- Magic responds to passion, conviction, fear, love, rage. The stronger the emotion, the stronger the magic. This is inherently volatile and makes the Fire Strand especially dangerous -- the very emotions that fuel power are the ones that corrode judgment.
- H) Other (describe)

**Guidance:** Your power source should create natural tension with the Crucible Structure. The best choice is one where using power MORE makes the protagonist's situation WORSE in at least one strand, even as it helps in another.

---

### Q9.3 — Power Limitations
**Override Type:** REPLACE

**Genre Options:**
- A) Physical cost -- exhaustion, pain, aging, bodily transformation -- Every use of magic taxes the body. Minor magic brings fatigue; major magic brings injury, premature aging, or permanent physical change. The protagonist's body is a ledger of their choices, and the debt compounds.
- B) Scarcity of magical sources or fuel -- Magic requires something to burn: rare minerals, specific herbs, blood, ambient magical energy that exists only in certain places. The quest for magical fuel becomes a strategic concern, and running out at the wrong moment is catastrophic.
- C) Corruption risk -- moral, spiritual, or physical -- Each use of magic brings the wielder closer to something they fear becoming. The corruption may be visible (physical transformation), spiritual (loss of empathy, growing cruelty), or metaphysical (attracting dark entities, weakening the veil between worlds).
- D) Required ritual conditions -- timing, location, components -- Magic cannot be used casually. It requires specific conditions: a full moon, a consecrated space, rare components consumed in casting, hours of preparation. This makes magic powerful but inflexible, and combat magic nearly impossible without preparation.
- E) Reciprocal cost -- equivalent exchange -- Magic obeys conservation. To create, you must destroy. To heal, something else must be harmed. To gain knowledge, you must forget something. The universe demands balance, and cheating the equation has terrible consequences.
- F) Psychological toll -- memory loss, personality erosion, madness -- Magic erodes the mind. Practitioners forget loved ones, lose emotional range, develop obsessions, or experience reality differently from others. The most powerful mages are often the least sane.
- G) Environmental impact -- each use damages the world -- Magic draws from the living world and leaves it diminished. Cast a fire spell, and the warmth is stolen from the surrounding area. Heal a wound, and nearby plants wither. Large-scale magic creates wastelands.
- H) Other (describe)

**Guidance:** Choose a limitation that directly threatens the Constellation Strand as well as the Fire Strand. The best limitations make the protagonist's power dangerous to those they love, not just to themselves. This creates the impossible choices that fuel Forge Points.
