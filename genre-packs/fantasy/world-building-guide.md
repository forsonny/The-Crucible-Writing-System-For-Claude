# Fantasy World-Building Guide

Comprehensive guidance for building fantasy worlds within the Crucible Writing System. This guide is used during the World Forge phase (Document 9) of planning and throughout the outlining and writing phases.

**Core Principle:** World-building serves story. Every element of the world should connect to at least one of the three strands (Quest, Fire, Constellation) or the thematic argument. World-building that exists only for its own sake belongs in an appendix, not in prose.

---

## Magic System Design

The magic system is the single most important world-building element in fantasy because it directly shapes the Fire Strand. Design it before anything else.

### Hard vs. Soft Magic

**Hard Magic** has explicit, well-defined rules that the reader understands.

| Aspect | Hard Magic |
|--------|-----------|
| Rules | Clear and consistent |
| Reader Knowledge | High -- readers can predict what is possible |
| Problem Solving | Magic can solve plot problems because rules are established |
| Sense of Wonder | Lower -- familiarity reduces mystery |
| Best For | Quest resolution, action sequences, protagonist's personal magic |
| Example Authors | Sanderson, Weeks, McClellan |

**Soft Magic** is mysterious, unexplained, and numinous.

| Aspect | Soft Magic |
|--------|-----------|
| Rules | Hidden, implied, or inconsistent |
| Reader Knowledge | Low -- readers cannot predict outcomes |
| Problem Solving | Should NOT solve plot problems (feels like deus ex machina) |
| Sense of Wonder | High -- mystery creates awe |
| Best For | Atmosphere, antagonist's power, ancient/divine magic, world texture |
| Example Authors | Tolkien, Le Guin, Martin |

**The Crucible Recommendation:** Use hard magic for the protagonist's Fire and soft magic for the world's deeper mysteries. This gives you clear dramatic stakes (the reader understands what power costs and what it can do) while preserving wonder (the world contains things beyond the protagonist's understanding).

### Sanderson's Three Laws in Depth

#### First Law: An author's ability to solve problems with magic is directly proportional to how well the reader understands that magic.

**Implementation Checklist:**
- [ ] The protagonist's magic is explained before it resolves any plot problem
- [ ] The costs of magic are demonstrated, not just stated
- [ ] The reader sees magic fail before they see it succeed at something critical
- [ ] The Apex resolution uses only established magical mechanics (or deliberately surrenders magic entirely)
- [ ] Beat 31 (Unexpected Agents) does not depend on unexplained magical intervention

**The Progression Pattern:**
1. **Movement I:** Magic is demonstrated. Reader sees what it does AND what it costs.
2. **Movement II:** Magic's limits are tested. Protagonist discovers what it cannot do.
3. **Movement III:** Magic's dark side is revealed. The cost deepens.
4. **Movement IV:** Magic is mastered (partially). Protagonist understands enough to use it deliberately.
5. **Movement V:** Magic is surrendered, transformed, or transcended. The Apex.

#### Second Law: Limitations are more interesting than powers.

**Implementation Checklist:**
- [ ] For every magical capability, there is at least one significant limitation
- [ ] Limitations create genuine dramatic tension, not just flavor
- [ ] The protagonist cannot solve Forge Points through magical power alone
- [ ] The Dark Mirror has found a way to circumvent (but not eliminate) the limitations, and this shortcut is what corrupted them

**Limitation Design Matrix:**

| Limitation | Narrative Function | Strand Affected |
|-----------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Physical exhaustion | Forces strategic use, creates vulnerability | Fire, Quest |
| Requires rare materials | Creates resource pressure, enables quest sub-goals | Quest |
| Takes time (ritual, preparation) | Prevents casual use, creates tension in combat | Quest |
| Corrupts the user | IS the Fire Strand | Fire |
| Harms bystanders | Threatens Constellation, enables Mercy moments | Constellation, Fire |
| Attracts predators/enemies | Creates escalating danger, ties to Quest stakes | Quest |
| Weakens over distance from source | Creates geographic constraints, enables quest logic | Quest |
| Requires emotional state | Links power to psychological health, creates vulnerability | Fire, Constellation |
| Cannot be used on the willing | Creates rules for consent, enables Dark Mirror's philosophy | All three |
| Each use is permanent | Every casting matters, no "spam magic" | Fire |

#### Third Law: Expand what you have before adding something new.

**Implementation Checklist:**
- [ ] No new magical mechanic introduced after the midpoint (FP2)
- [ ] "New" abilities are extensions of established ones, not separate systems
- [ ] If the world has multiple magic systems, the protagonist engages primarily with one
- [ ] The climax uses the same magic shown in the opening, but wielded with greater understanding

### Magic System Design Template

Use this template during the World Forge phase:

```
MAGIC SYSTEM: [Name]

SOURCE: Where does power come from?
MECHANISM: How is it accessed/channeled?
EFFECTS: What can it do? (List 3-5 core capabilities)
LIMITATIONS: What can it NOT do? (List at least as many as effects)
COST: What does each use take from the wielder?
PROGRESSION: How does skill/power grow?
CORRUPTION: What happens when limits are pushed?
CULTURAL ROLE: How does society view this magic?
PROTAGONIST'S RELATIONSHIP: How does the protagonist access/experience it?
DARK MIRROR'S RELATIONSHIP: How has the antagonist subverted or mastered it?
```

## Fantasy Geography and Ecology

### Building a World Map

Fantasy geography should serve the Quest Strand. The protagonist's journey across the map IS the quest's physical expression.

**Key Geographic Considerations:**

| Element | Narrative Function | Example |
|---------|-------------------|---------|
| Natural barriers | Create quest obstacles and chapter boundaries | Mountain ranges, oceans, deserts, cursed wastelands |
| Travel corridors | Enable journey narrative and pacing | River valleys, trade roads, mountain passes |
| Magical anomalies | Create zones where rules change | Dead magic zones, wild magic storms, ley line intersections |
| Resource geography | Justify political structures and conflicts | Mineral deposits, fertile land, magical wellsprings |
| Climate zones | Provide sensory variety and challenge progression | Protagonist moves from temperate home to extreme environment |

**The Journey Pattern:**
Most fantasy Quest Strands involve physical movement across the world. Design your map so the protagonist's geographical journey mirrors their internal journey (Fire Strand):

- **Starting point:** Safe, familiar, limited
- **Early journey:** Challenging but navigable, new wonders
- **Middle journey:** Dangerous, alien, testing
- **Approach to climax:** Hostile, corrupted, reflecting the Dark Mirror's influence
- **Climax location:** The most extreme environment, where everything is at stake

### Ecology and the Magic System

If magic exists, it shapes the ecosystem:

- **Magical flora:** Plants that absorb ambient magic, produce magical effects, or respond to spellcasting. These can provide potion ingredients, serve as navigational markers (they grow near ley lines), or create hazards.
- **Magical fauna:** Creatures adapted to magical environments. Predators that feed on magical energy. Prey animals that use magical camouflage. Symbiotic relationships between creatures and magic users.
- **Environmental magic:** Weather affected by magical events. Seasons tied to magical cycles. Natural disasters as magical phenomena.

**Rule of Consequence:** If magic exists, EVERYTHING in the world should be shaped by it. An ecosystem that evolved alongside magic for millennia will not look like Earth with dragons added.

## Social Structures

### Political Systems

Fantasy political structures are shaped by magic. A world with powerful individual magic users will not develop the same political systems as a world with no magic.

| If Magic Is... | Political Structure Tends Toward... |
|----------------|--------------------------------------|
| Rare and powerful | Magocracy or magic-users as advisors/weapons to kings |
| Common and weak | Democratic or guild-based, magic as profession |
| Hereditary | Aristocracy reinforced by magical bloodlines |
| Learned/studied | Meritocratic academies with political influence |
| Divine/granted | Theocracy or priestly class |
| Wild/natural | Tribal, shamanic, decentralized |
| Artificed/crafted | Mercantile, guild-controlled, proto-industrial |

### Kingdoms and Empires

When building political entities:

- **Why does this polity exist?** Geography, shared magical tradition, conquest, alliance against common threat
- **What holds it together?** Loyalty, fear, trade dependence, magical bonds, shared religion
- **What threatens to tear it apart?** Succession disputes, magical inequality, resource scarcity, the story's central conflict
- **How does it relate to the Quest?** Ally, obstacle, battleground, or prize

### Guilds, Orders, and Magical Institutions

Organized magic creates institutions. Institutions create politics, which creates Constellation Strand material.

**Types of Magical Organizations:**

| Type | Structure | Narrative Role |
|------|-----------|---------------|
| Academy/University | Hierarchical, meritocratic, knowledge-hoarding | Training ground, political player, potential betrayer |
| Martial Order | Military discipline, oaths, honor code | Constellation allies, sources of duty vs. desire conflict |
| Priesthood/Temple | Devotional, ritualistic, community-serving | Moral authority, potential opposition to protagonist's methods |
| Secret Society | Hidden, selective, agenda-driven | Source of hidden knowledge, conspiracy, betrayal |
| Merchant Guild | Profit-driven, pragmatic, networked | Resource access, moral compromise, real-world grounding |
| Wild Tradition | Informal, master-apprentice, nature-connected | Alternative perspective, anti-institutional voice |

### Races and Species

If your world includes non-human species:

- **Each species should have a distinct relationship to magic.** Not just "elves use nature magic" -- how does their biology, lifespan, culture, and philosophy interact with the magical system?
- **Avoid racial essentialism.** Individual members of any species should be capable of the full range of moral choice. The Dark Mirror requirement demands that even the most alien antagonist has comprehensible motivation.
- **Cultural diversity within species.** Humans are not monolithic; neither should any other species be.
- **Biological realism (as much as magic allows).** If a species is very long-lived, their politics, economics, and psychology should reflect that. If a species is physically large, their architecture, agriculture, and warfare should accommodate it.

## Religion and Mythology

### Gods and the Divine

Religious systems in fantasy must answer one critical question: **Are the gods real and active?**

| Divine Status | World Implications | Narrative Effect |
|---------------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Active and interventionist | Miracles happen, divine politics matter | Quest may involve divine mandate or opposition |
| Real but distant | Created the world, then withdrew | "Why have the gods abandoned us?" drives tension |
| Dead or dying | Their remains/echoes persist | The World's Wound may be divine death |
| Contested/ambiguous | Believers and skeptics coexist | Thematic richness, multiple interpretations |
| False (purely cultural) | Religion is human construct | Political and social power dynamics |
| One true faith among many false | Creates religious conflict | Crusade/heresy narratives, inquisition |

### Mythology as World History

Fantasy mythology often IS history, just distorted by time:

- **Creation myths** encode actual cosmological events
- **Hero legends** reference real previous wielders (Q9.4)
- **Cautionary tales** preserve warnings about magical dangers
- **Prophecies** may be genuine foresight or self-fulfilling narratives

**Crucible Connection:** The Dark Mirror often has a different interpretation of the same mythology. Both protagonist and antagonist may claim the same legend supports their philosophy. This is the "Kernel of Truth" in mythological form.

### Designing a Pantheon

If your world has multiple deities:

- Each god should represent a domain that connects to the story's themes
- At least one deity should be associated with the Fire's power source
- The gods' relationships (allies, enemies, betrayers) should mirror the story's relational dynamics
- Divine absence, silence, or failure creates powerful dramatic tension

## Economy and Trade

### Magical Commerce

A world with magic has a magical economy. What is magic worth? Who controls it?

**Economic Questions to Answer:**

| Question | Implication |
|----------|-------------|
| Can magic be bought and sold? | Commodification of the supernatural, class implications |
| Are magical services affordable? | Magical healing available to the rich but not the poor creates social tension |
| What replaces technology? | If magic can light rooms, why develop electricity? What CANNOT magic replace? |
| Is there magical currency? | Enchanted coins, stored spell-energy, mana crystals as medium of exchange |
| What is the most valuable magical commodity? | This will be strategically important in the Quest Strand |
| Who controls magical resources? | Whoever controls the source of magic controls the world |

### Non-Magical Economy

Do not forget the mundane economy. Most people in most fantasy worlds are not magic users. They need:

- Food production (farming, herding, hunting, fishing, foraging)
- Shelter and construction (how are buildings made? does magic assist?)
- Trade goods (what does each region produce that others need?)
- Currency (coins, barter, credit, magical equivalent)
- Labor (who does the work? are there enslaved populations? magical labor?)

**Crucible Connection:** The Constellation Strand benefits enormously from economic grounding. Common folk allies (Q4.4, Option D) are more vivid when their economic reality is clear. The innkeeper who shelters the party has a business to protect. The smith who forges weapons has a family to feed.

## Military and Warfare

### Conventional Warfare

Military structure in fantasy should reflect the political and magical reality:

| If Magic Is... | Warfare Looks Like... |
|----------------|----------------------|
| Rare, powerful | Individual mages as strategic assets, like nuclear weapons |
| Common, moderate | Magical units integrated into conventional armies |
| Ubiquitous, weak | Magic replaces some conventional weapons/tools |
| Restricted to elites | Magical aristocratic warrior class, magical cavalry/knights |
| Forbidden/feared | Conventional warfare; magic users are secret weapons or war crimes |

### Magical Warfare

Design magical combat with the same rigor as the magic system itself:

- **Scale:** Can one mage destroy an army? If so, why hasn't one already?
- **Countermeasures:** How is magic defended against? Wards, anti-magic, resistant materials, simply being out of range?
- **Logistics:** Do mages need rest, supplies, specific conditions? Magical logistics are as important as food and arrows.
- **Casualties:** What does a magical battlefield look like afterward? Are there lasting effects? Cursed ground, lingering enchantments, magical radiation?
- **Rules of war:** Are there things magic users agree not to do? If so, who broke the agreement and what happened?

### Personal Combat

If the protagonist fights:

- **Establish their combat style early** and let it evolve as their Fire grows
- **Magic in combat should cost something visible** -- readers should see the protagonist paying the price in real time
- **Avoid power scaling that makes early threats irrelevant** -- if the protagonist can kill a dragon by Act III, how do you threaten them in Act V?
- **The best fights are won through cleverness, sacrifice, or character** -- not raw power. The Mercy Engine applies even in combat.

## History and Lore

### Building a Timeline

Fantasy worlds need history. The depth of that history depends on your subgenre:

| Subgenre | Historical Depth Needed |
|----------|------------------------|
| Epic Fantasy | Deep -- multiple ages, fallen civilizations, legendary heroes |
| High Fantasy | Moderate to deep -- creation myths, at least one previous age |
| Low Fantasy | Shallow to moderate -- recent political history, folklore |
| Dark Fantasy | Moderate -- enough to explain how things got this bad |
| Heroic Fantasy | Shallow -- legends and rumors, not detailed chronicles |

### Ages and Epochs

Many fantasy worlds are structured in "ages" -- distinct historical periods separated by cataclysmic events:

**Common Pattern:**
1. **Age of Creation/Gods** -- The world is made, often imperfectly
2. **Golden Age** -- A period of wonder, often when magic was at its height
3. **The Cataclysm/Fall** -- Something went terribly wrong (often magical hubris)
4. **Recovery/Current Age** -- The world rebuilt, but diminished
5. **The Present Crisis** -- The story begins here; echoes of the Fall are returning

**Crucible Connection:** The World's Wound (Q9.1) is often rooted in historical catastrophe. The protagonist's quest may be to finally resolve what a previous age left unfinished. The Dark Mirror may be trying to restore a previous age at any cost.

### Fallen Civilizations

Ruins are a fantasy staple because they serve multiple narrative functions:

- **Quest locations:** Ancient ruins contain knowledge, artifacts, or sealed evils
- **World-building shorthand:** Ruins show what the world was and what it lost
- **Fire Strand symbolism:** The protagonist walks through the remains of those who had the same power and failed
- **Mercy Engine opportunities:** Spirits, survivors, or creatures living in ruins can be objects of mercy

### The Lore Delivery Problem

History must reach the reader without lectures. Methods that work:

| Method | Best For | Risk |
|--------|----------|------|
| Character who knows history | Mentor figures, scholars in the Constellation | Lectures; "as you know, Bob" |
| Discovery through exploration | Ruins, ancient texts, inscriptions | Pacing drag if overdone |
| Legends and stories within the story | Campfire tales, songs, cultural references | Distraction from main narrative |
| Protagonist's direct experience | Visions, magical memory, time magic | Can feel contrived |
| Gradual revelation through events | History repeating; current events explained by past | Requires careful planning |
| Antagonist's perspective | Dark Mirror reveals history protagonist does not know | Risk of villain monologue |

**The Rule:** No more than three paragraphs of pure historical exposition in sequence. After three paragraphs, a character must react, an event must interrupt, or a scene must shift.

## Languages and Naming Conventions

### Constructed Languages

Full constructed languages (conlangs) are rarely necessary. What matters is consistency.

**Minimum Viable Language:**
- A phonetic system (which sounds does this language favor?)
- A naming convention (how are people/places named?)
- A few key words or phrases that appear in the narrative
- Enough consistency that readers can "hear" the language's character

### Naming Conventions

Names do enormous subconscious work. Readers form instant impressions based on phonetics.

| Sound Quality | Impression | Example Names |
|---------------|-----------|---------------|
| Hard consonants (K, T, D, G) | Strength, harshness, aggression | Karak, Theron, Grim, Drakon |
| Soft consonants (L, M, N, S) | Grace, wisdom, gentleness | Lorien, Maelis, Selene, Nimue |
| Long vowels (AH, EE, OO) | Dignity, age, formality | Aethon, Seeren, Morune |
| Short vowels (A, E, I) | Energy, youth, sharpness | Vin, Kira, Tam, Brek |
| Sibilants (S, SH, Z) | Mystery, danger, magic | Shael, Zephira, Sserin |

**Cultural Consistency:** All names from the same culture should share phonetic DNA. Readers should be able to tell, without being told, that Kira and Tam are from the same culture, and that Lord Sethren is from a different one.

**Naming Pitfalls:**
- Apostrophes in names (Kha'reth, Tel'vaan) are a crutch. Use sparingly or not at all.
- Names that are too similar cause confusion (Saren and Soren, Maric and Malik)
- Names that are unpronounceable break immersion. If the reader cannot say it aloud, simplify it.
- Names with obvious real-world origins in a secondary world (a character named "Steve" in a pseudo-medieval setting) shatter tone unless deliberately comedic.

### Place Names

Place names should reflect the culture that named the location:

- **Descriptive names** for common folk locations: Millbridge, Ironford, Thornwall
- **Historical names** for ancient places: named after founders, events, or lost languages
- **Magical names** for enchanted locations: names that carry power or are feared to speak aloud
- **Corrupted names** for fallen places: names that were once beautiful, now twisted

## Flora and Fauna

### Magical Creatures

Magical creatures should obey the logic of the magic system and the ecosystem:

**Design Principles:**
- **Ecological niche:** What does this creature eat? What eats it? Where does it live?
- **Magical relationship:** How does it interact with the magic system? Is it a source, a consumer, or indifferent?
- **Narrative function:** Why does this creature exist in the story? What strand does it serve?
- **Cultural meaning:** How do people in the world regard this creature? Feared, revered, hunted, domesticated?

### Common Fantasy Creature Roles

| Role | Function | Example Creatures |
|------|----------|-------------------|
| Mount | Transportation, bond with rider, combat asset | Dragons, griffins, giant wolves, magical horses |
| Familiar/Companion | Emotional bond, magical conduit, Constellation member | Cats, ravens, spirits, small dragons |
| Predator/Hazard | Quest obstacle, environmental danger | Basilisks, wyrms, magical storms embodied |
| Guardian | Protects locations or objects, must be overcome or befriended | Golems, bound spirits, ancient beasts |
| Resource | Provides magical materials, food, or services | Silk-worms that produce enchanted thread, beasts with alchemical components |
| Messenger/Guide | Provides information, leads the way | Spirit animals, will-o-wisps, prophetic birds |
| Symbol | Embodies theme, reflects protagonist's state | A creature that thrives or sickens with the protagonist's choices |

### Flora

Magical plants are often overlooked but provide rich world-building:

- **Alchemical ingredients** create quest sub-goals and economic reality
- **Magical forests** are classic locations with deep mythic resonance
- **Crops affected by magic** ground the world in agricultural reality
- **Healing herbs** provide non-magical solutions (reinforcing the Mercy Engine's theme)
- **Corrupted or dying vegetation** as visible sign of the World's Wound

## How the World Forge Maps to Fantasy World-Building

The World Forge planning document (Document 9) creates the structural skeleton of your world. Here is how each World Forge question maps to the detailed world-building categories in this guide.

### Q9.1 (World's Wound) Maps To:

- **History:** The wound often originates in a historical event (a previous age's catastrophe, a god's death, a hero's failure)
- **Geography:** The wound manifests physically (spreading corruption, dying lands, a literal wound in the earth)
- **Ecology:** Flora and fauna respond to the wound (creatures sicken, plants die, magical beasts flee or go mad)
- **Religion:** The wound may challenge theological assumptions (if the gods are good, why is the world dying?)

**Action:** After answering Q9.1, develop the wound's historical origin, its physical manifestation, and its ecological effects.

### Q9.2 (Power Source) Maps To:

- **Magic System:** This IS your magic system's foundation. Everything flows from here.
- **Social Structure:** Who has access to the power source determines political structure
- **Economy:** The power source shapes what is valuable and who controls wealth
- **Military:** The power source determines the nature of magical warfare

**Action:** After answering Q9.2, design the full magic system using the template in this guide.

### Q9.3 (Power Limitations) Maps To:

- **Magic System:** Limitations are as important as capabilities
- **Fire Strand:** The protagonist's limitations create the Fire's dramatic tension
- **Economy:** Limitations create scarcity, which creates economic value
- **Culture:** How society views and manages limitations shapes cultural attitudes toward magic

**Action:** After answering Q9.3, develop at least three specific limitations with distinct narrative consequences.

### Q9.4 (Previous Wielders) Maps To:

- **History:** Previous wielders ARE history. Their actions shaped the world.
- **Lore:** Stories about previous wielders provide myths, warnings, and prophecy
- **Locations:** Previous wielders left marks on the landscape (ruins, magical anomalies, sealed places)
- **Dark Mirror:** The antagonist may have a specific relationship to a previous wielder

**Action:** After answering Q9.4, write a brief paragraph about each notable previous wielder: who they were, what they did, and how they fell or succeeded.

### Q9.5 (Key Locations) Maps To:

- **Geography:** Locations create the map
- **Ecology:** Each location has its own environmental character
- **Culture:** Each location has its own cultural flavor
- **Quest Strand:** The protagonist's journey connects locations in sequence

**Action:** After answering Q9.5, develop each location with at least: a physical description, a cultural character, a magical quality, and a narrative function.

### Q9.6 (World-Protagonist Mirror) Maps To:

- **Ecology:** The natural world responds to the protagonist
- **Geography:** Landscapes shift or change
- **Magic System:** Magical effects intensify or weaken
- **Theme:** The world-mirror is a direct expression of the story's thematic argument

**Action:** After answering Q9.6, define three specific ways the world visibly responds to the protagonist's state: one at the beginning (subtle), one at the midpoint (noticeable), one at the climax (dramatic).

### Q9.7 (Cultures) Maps To:

- **Social Structure:** Each culture has its own political system
- **Religion:** Each culture has its own relationship to the divine
- **Languages:** Each culture has its own naming conventions and speech patterns
- **Economy:** Cultures trade, compete, and specialize

**Action:** After answering Q9.7, develop each culture with at least: a relationship to magic, a political structure, a value system, and a naming convention.

### Q9.8 (History) Maps To:

- **History and Lore:** This IS the historical framework
- **Previous Wielders:** History includes the stories of those who held power before
- **Religion:** History shapes theology and mythology
- **Locations:** Historical events created ruins, battlefields, sacred sites

**Action:** After answering Q9.8, create a brief timeline with three to five key events that shaped the current world.

## World-Building Checklist

Before moving from the World Forge to outlining, verify:

### Magic System
- [ ] Source of magic is defined
- [ ] At least three specific capabilities are listed
- [ ] At least three specific limitations are listed
- [ ] Cost of use is clear and consistent
- [ ] Corruption/transformation path is defined
- [ ] Hard/soft magic balance is chosen
- [ ] Magic system connects to Fire Strand mechanically

### Geography
- [ ] Key locations from Q9.5 are placed in spatial relationship
- [ ] The protagonist's journey makes geographic sense
- [ ] At least one location exists for each movement of the story
- [ ] Environmental variety exists (not all forests, not all cities)

### Social and Political
- [ ] At least one political structure is defined
- [ ] Magic's effect on social hierarchy is considered
- [ ] The Constellation's members have a social context (where do they come from? what class/role?)

### History
- [ ] At least three historical events are defined
- [ ] Previous wielders of power are described
- [ ] The current crisis connects to historical events
- [ ] The Dark Mirror's grievance or philosophy has historical roots

### Culture
- [ ] Naming conventions are consistent within each culture
- [ ] At least one non-human perspective exists (if applicable)
- [ ] Dialogue register reflects cultural background
- [ ] Religion or belief systems are sketched

### Ecology
- [ ] The World's Wound has visible environmental effects
- [ ] At least one magical creature or plant is defined
- [ ] The ecosystem responds to magic (it is not just Earth with spells)

### Economy
- [ ] Basic economic reality is established (how do people eat, trade, survive?)
- [ ] Magic's economic impact is considered
- [ ] Resource scarcity relevant to the Quest is identified

## A Final Note on Restraint

The greatest temptation in fantasy world-building is to build too much. A fully realized world with twelve cultures, three magic systems, a complete constructed language, and four thousand years of recorded history is impressive -- but if it all ends up in the prose, the story suffocates under its own weight.

**The Iceberg Principle:** Build ten times what you show. Know the history, but let only the relevant fragments surface. Understand the economy, but mention it only when characters interact with it. Design the language, but use only the words that earn their place in dialogue.

The world exists to serve the Crucible. The Quest must have somewhere to go. The Fire must have a system to burn within. The Constellation must have a society to inhabit. Everything else is foundation -- felt by the reader but never seen.

Build deep. Show wisely. Trust the reader to sense the depth beneath.
